4.1Gathering, Organizing, Representing, Analyzing and Interpreting Data

What are the commuting patterns of RTU students? You cannot answer that by asking one student, and you cannot ask all of them either. Statistics is the science of collecting, organizing, representing, analyzing and interpreting data so that we can make good decisions when we are uncertain. This chapter follows one running example, a survey of how RTU students get to campus, through all five stages.

Gather → Organize → Represent → Analyze → Interpret (and decide)

1. Gathering data. Start with a question that data can answer. The population is the entire group we want to learn about (all RTU students). A sample is the smaller group we actually observe (10 students interviewed by your group). We study the sample to learn about the population, so the sample must represent it fairly.

Definition A parameter describes a population (the true mean commute of all RTU students, \(\mu\)); a statistic describes a sample (the mean commute of your 10 respondents, \(\bar{x}\)). We use statistics to estimate parameters.

Sampling methods. In a simple random sample every member has an equal chance of selection (draw student numbers by lottery). A systematic sample takes every \(k\)th member from a list or line (every 10th student through the gate). A stratified sample divides the population into groups (colleges or year levels) and samples randomly within each. A cluster sample randomly picks whole groups (entire blocks) and surveys everyone in them. A convenience sample (only your friends in the canteen) is easy but usually biased.

Types of data. Qualitative (categorical) data describe qualities or categories: mode of transportation (jeepney, MRT, tricycle, TNVS), program, city of residence. Quantitative data are numbers that measure or count: travel time, distance, fare, number of transfers. Quantitative data are discrete if they take countable values (number of transfers: 0, 1, 2, …) and continuous if they can take any value in an interval (travel time 43.27 minutes, distance 8.5 km).

Common mistake Calling data quantitative just because they are written with digits. Student numbers, ZIP codes and jeepney route numbers are labels, not measurements: averaging them means nothing, so they are qualitative.

2. Organizing data. Raw data such as "50, 45, 120, 75, 90, 30, 15, 60, 25, 80" are hard to read. First arrange observations in a data table: one row per respondent, one column per variable. Then summarize with a frequency table, which lists each category or value with the number of times it occurs. For numerical data with many values, group them into class intervals (0–29 min, 30–59 min, …). The relative frequency of a class is its frequency divided by the total.

3. Representing data. Choose the graph that fits the data. A bar graph compares categories (separated bars). A pie chart shows parts of a whole (percentages adding to 100%). A histogram shows the distribution of grouped numerical data (bars touch because the intervals are continuous). A scatterplot shows the relationship between two quantitative variables, such as distance from RTU and travel time (Section 4.6).

4–5. Analyzing and interpreting. Analysis means computing summaries: where the centre is (Section 4.2), how spread out the data are (4.3), where an individual stands (4.4), how likely events are (4.5) and how variables are related (4.6). Interpretation means stating what the numbers say about the original question, in words, with their limitations: "With only 10 respondents, our conclusions about all RTU students are tentative."

Common mistake Using a pie chart for data that are not parts of one whole (for example, the average fare of each group), or drawing a histogram with gaps between bars. Gaps belong to bar graphs of categories.

Ethics. When you collect data from people, introduce yourself, explain how the data will be used, do not record names (use codes like G1R01), and let respondents withdraw. Honest reporting, including the data that do not fit your expectations, is part of doing statistics with integrity.

4.2Measures of Central Tendency

A measure of central tendency is a single number that represents the "typical" or central value of a data set. The three most common are the mean, the median and the mode. Each answers "what is typical?" in a different way, and choosing well is part of the skill.

Key equations
\[ \bar{x} = \frac{\sum x}{n} \;\text{(sample mean)}, \qquad \mu = \frac{\sum x}{N} \;\text{(population mean)} \]
\[ \bar{x}_w = \frac{\sum w x}{\sum w} \;\text{(weighted mean)} \]

Mean. Add all values and divide by how many there are. For the commute times 15, 25, 30, 45, 50, 60, 75, 80, 90, 120 minutes, \(\sum x = 590\) and \(\bar{x} = 590/10 = 59\) minutes. The mean uses every value, which is its strength and its weakness: one extreme value (an outlier) pulls it strongly. Best for roughly symmetric numerical data without extreme outliers.

Median. The middle value when the data are in order. If \(n\) is odd, it is the value in position \((n + 1)/2\); if \(n\) is even, it is the mean of the two middle values. For the commute data (\(n = 10\)) the middle values are the 5th and 6th, 50 and 60, so the median is \((50 + 60)/2 = 55\) minutes. The median ignores how far the extreme values are, so it is resistant to outliers. Best for skewed data and data with outliers (incomes, house prices, commute times).

Mode. The value or category that occurs most often. A data set can have no mode, one mode, or several (bimodal, multimodal). For transportation, the mode is "jeepney". The mode is the only measure of centre for qualitative data, where you cannot add or order the values.

Example Daily fares (pesos) of eight students: 40, 55, 60, 60, 75, 90, 110, 150. Mean \(= 640/8 = 80\); median \(= (60 + 75)/2 = 67.5\); mode \(= 60\). The single large fare of 150 pulls the mean above most of the data (6 of 8 students pay less than 80), so the median better describes a typical fare.

Weighted mean. When values do not count equally, multiply each by its weight. Your General Weighted Average (GWA) weights each grade by the course's units. For grades 1.25 (3 units), 1.75 (3 units), 2.00 (2 units), 1.00 (1 unit) and 1.50 (5 units): \(\sum wx = 3.75 + 5.25 + 4.00 + 1.00 + 7.50 = 21.50\), \(\sum w = 14\), so GWA \(= 21.50/14 \approx 1.54\).

Shape and the three measures. In a symmetric distribution the mean, median and mode are about equal. In a right-skewed distribution (a long tail of large values, like commutes where a few students travel two hours) the mean is pulled toward the tail: mode < median < mean. In a left-skewed distribution the order reverses.

Common mistake Finding the median without first sorting the data, or forgetting to average the two middle values when \(n\) is even. In the unsorted list 50, 45, 120, 75, 90, … the "middle" entries mean nothing.

4.3Measures of Dispersion

Two jeepney routes can both average 40 minutes, yet one takes 38 to 42 minutes every day while the other swings from 15 to 90. The average alone hides this difference. Measures of dispersion (variability) describe how spread out the data are.

Range \(=\) highest value \(-\) lowest value. For the commute data, \(120 - 15 = 105\) minutes. It is quick but depends only on the two extremes.

Key equations
\[ \sigma^2 = \frac{\sum (x - \mu)^2}{N}, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2} \quad \text{(population)} \]
\[ s^2 = \frac{\sum (x - \bar{x})^2}{n - 1}, \qquad s = \sqrt{s^2} \quad \text{(sample)} \]
\[ \text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1 \]

Variance and standard deviation. Measure how far each value is from the mean (its deviation \(x - \bar{x}\)), square the deviations so negatives do not cancel positives, and average them. That average of squared deviations is the variance; its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the original units. For a sample we divide by \(n - 1\) (the degrees of freedom) instead of \(n\); this corrects the tendency of a sample to underestimate the population's spread.

The deviations always add to 0, a useful check. Sample variance \(s^2 = 9590/9 \approx 1065.56\) square minutes, and \(s = \sqrt{1065.56} \approx 32.64\) minutes. Interpretation: "The average travel time is 59 minutes, with a standard deviation of about 32.64 minutes." A small standard deviation means the data cluster tightly around the mean; a large one means they are widely spread. (If these 10 students were the whole population, \(\sigma = \sqrt{9590/10} \approx 30.97\) minutes.)

Interquartile range. The IQR measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data, so it ignores the extremes. Sort the data and split it into a lower and an upper half (if \(n\) is odd, leave the median out of both halves). \(Q_1\) is the median of the lower half, \(Q_3\) the median of the upper half. Commute data: lower half 15, 25, 30, 45, 50 gives \(Q_1 = 30\); upper half 60, 75, 80, 90, 120 gives \(Q_3 = 80\); IQR \(= 80 - 30 = 50\) minutes. Pair the IQR with the median for skewed data; pair the standard deviation with the mean for symmetric data.

Common mistake Dividing by \(n\) when the data are a sample, or forgetting the square root and reporting the variance (in squared units) as the standard deviation. Also note: the variance and standard deviation are never negative.

Note: calculators and spreadsheets use slightly different rules for quartiles, so they may give \(Q_1\) and \(Q_3\) a little different from the "median of each half" method used in this course. Use the course method in activities.

4.4Measures of Relative Position

Where does one student's commute stand compared with everyone else's? Measures of relative position locate a single value within a data set.

Quartiles \(Q_1, Q_2, Q_3\) divide ordered data into four parts with about 25% of the data in each; \(Q_2\) is the median. Percentiles divide the data into 100 parts: the \(k\)th percentile \(P_k\) is the value below which about \(k\%\) of the data fall, so \(Q_1 = P_{25}\), \(Q_2 = P_{50}\), \(Q_3 = P_{75}\). Board exam results, growth charts for children and admission tests are often reported as percentiles.

Key equations
\[ z = \frac{x - \bar{x}}{s} \;\text{(sample)}, \qquad z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \;\text{(population)} \]
\[ \text{percentile rank of } x = \frac{\text{number of values below } x}{n} \times 100 \]

Percentile rank answers the reverse question: given a value, what percent of the data are below it? In the commute data, 8 of the 10 values are below 90 minutes, so 90 minutes has a percentile rank of \(8/10 \times 100 = 80\): it is at the 80th percentile. (Some books count half of the values equal to \(x\) as well; we use "values below" in this course.)

The z-score (standard score) tells how many standard deviations a value lies above (positive) or below (negative) the mean. For 90 minutes: \(z = (90 - 59)/32.64 \approx 0.95\). For 15 minutes: \(z = (15 - 59)/32.64 \approx -1.35\). Because z-scores have no units, they let us compare values from different data sets: a score of 85 on a test with mean 72 and standard deviation 8 (\(z = 1.625\)) is relatively better than 90 on a test with mean 85 and standard deviation 5 (\(z = 1\)).

Example Interpreting both measures for one respondent: "A 90-minute commute is about 0.95 standard deviations above the average and longer than 80% of the commutes in our sample."

The five-number summary and box plot. Minimum, \(Q_1\), median, \(Q_3\), maximum: for the commute data 15, 30, 55, 80, 120. A box plot draws a box from \(Q_1\) to \(Q_3\) with a line at the median, and whiskers out to the minimum and maximum. A value more than \(1.5 \times \text{IQR}\) below \(Q_1\) or above \(Q_3\) is usually flagged as an outlier; here the fences are \(30 - 75 = -45\) and \(80 + 75 = 155\), so there are no outliers.

Common mistake Confusing a percentile with a percentage score. Scoring at the 80th percentile on an exam does not mean you got 80% of the items right; it means you scored higher than about 80% of the examinees.

4.5Probabilities and the Normal Distribution

How likely is a long commute? Probability is a number from 0 to 1 that measures how likely an event \(E\) is to happen. 0 means impossible, 0.5 means as likely as not, 1 means certain.

Key equations
\[ P(E) = \frac{\text{number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}, \qquad 0 \le P(E) \le 1 \]
\[ P(\text{not } E) = 1 - P(E) \quad \text{(complement rule)} \]

When outcomes are equally likely (a fair die, a fair coin) we compute theoretical probability: \(P(\text{even number on a die}) = 3/6 = 0.5\). When we compute probability from collected data it is empirical (relative frequency) probability. From our survey, 5 of 10 students ride a jeepney, so \(P(\text{jeepney}) = 5/10 = 0.5\); 4 of 10 commute more than 60 minutes, so \(P(\text{more than 60 min}) = 0.4\) and \(P(\text{60 min or less}) = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6\).

The normal distribution. Many measurements (heights, exam scores, measurement errors, and approximately our commute times) follow a bell-shaped curve called the normal distribution. Its properties: it is symmetric about the mean; mean = median = mode; the total area under the curve is 1 (100%); and the area under the curve over an interval equals the probability of a value in that interval.

The empirical rule (68–95–99.7 rule) gives quick estimates. If board exam scores are normal with mean 75 and standard deviation 8, about 68% of examinees score between 67 and 83, about 95% between 59 and 91, and nearly all between 51 and 99. By symmetry, about \((100 - 68)/2 = 16\%\) score above 83.

Using the z-table. For other values, convert to a z-score and read the area to the left of \(z\) from a standard normal table. Some values:

Common mistake Reading the table value as the answer for a "greater than" question. The table gives the area to the left; for \(P(X > x)\) you must subtract from 1. Always sketch the curve and shade the area you want first.

4.6Correlation and Regression

Does living farther from RTU mean a longer commute? To study the relationship between two quantitative variables, first draw a scatterplot, with the explanatory variable \(x\) (distance) on the horizontal axis and the response variable \(y\) (travel time) on the vertical axis. Then measure the relationship with correlation, and model it with a regression line.

Definition The Pearson correlation coefficient \(r\) measures the strength and direction of the linear relationship between \(x\) and \(y\). It ranges from \(-1\) to \(+1\): \(r > 0\) positive (as \(x\) increases, \(y\) tends to increase), \(r < 0\) negative, \(r \approx 0\) no linear relationship.
Key equations
\[ r = \frac{n\sum xy - \sum x \sum y}{\sqrt{\left[n\sum x^2 - (\sum x)^2\right]\left[n\sum y^2 - (\sum y)^2\right]}} \]
\[ b = \frac{n\sum xy - \sum x\sum y}{n\sum x^2 - (\sum x)^2}, \qquad a = \bar{y} - b\bar{x}, \qquad \hat{y} = a + bx \]

The commute data. Ten respondents gave distance \(x\) (km) and travel time \(y\) (min): (4, 20), (5, 35), (10, 60), (13, 75), (17, 60), (17, 90), (18, 75), (19, 80), (22, 100), (25, 130). The totals are \(n = 10\), \(\sum x = 150\), \(\sum y = 725\), \(\sum xy = 12\,700\), \(\sum x^2 = 2682\), \(\sum y^2 = 61\,475\). Then

\[ r = \frac{10(12\,700) - 150(725)}{\sqrt{[10(2682) - 150^2][10(61\,475) - 725^2]}} = \frac{18\,250}{\sqrt{(4320)(89\,125)}} \approx 0.93 \]

a very strong positive correlation: students who live farther tend to have longer commutes.

Simple linear regression finds the least-squares line \(\hat{y} = a + bx\), the line that makes the sum of squared vertical distances from the points to the line as small as possible. Here \(b = 18\,250/4320 \approx 4.22\) and \(a = 72.5 - 4.22(15) \approx 9.13\) (using unrounded \(b\)), so \(\hat{y} = 9.13 + 4.22x\). The slope means each additional kilometre adds about 4.22 minutes; the intercept is the model's travel time at 0 km (time spent walking, waiting, transferring). For a student 12 km away we predict \(9.13 + 4.22(12) \approx 59.8\) minutes.

The coefficient of determination \(r^2\) is the fraction of the variation in \(y\) explained by the line. Here \(r^2 \approx 0.865\): distance explains about 87% of the variation in travel time; the rest comes from traffic, mode of transport, weather and so on.

Common mistake Correlation does not mean causation. Ice cream sales and drowning incidents are positively correlated, but both are caused by hot weather (a lurking variable). Also, do not extrapolate: our data run from 4 to 25 km, so predicting the commute of a student 80 km away with this line is unreliable.

Interpret and decide. Putting the chapter together for our survey: the typical commute is about an hour but varies a lot (\(s \approx 33\) min); the jeepney is the most common mode; the normal model predicts about 17% of students need more than 90 minutes; and distance strongly predicts travel time. Decision: a student living 20 km away should plan for about \(9.13 + 4.22(20) \approx 94\) minutes, so leave at least an hour and a half before class. Limitation: \(n = 10\) is small; combining all groups' data would give more reliable conclusions.

★Key takeaways

  • Statistics moves through five stages: gather, organize, represent, analyze, interpret and decide.
  • We study a sample to learn about a population; a fair (random) sample matters more than a large biased one.
  • Mean for symmetric data, median for skewed data or outliers, mode for categories.
  • Standard deviation measures the typical distance from the mean; divide by n − 1 for a sample. The IQR measures the spread of the middle 50%.
  • A z-score tells how many standard deviations a value is from the mean; percentile rank tells what percent of the data lie below it.
  • Normal curve: 68–95–99.7 within 1, 2, 3 standard deviations; areas under the curve are probabilities.
  • Pearson's r measures linear strength and direction; the regression line ŷ = a + bx predicts y within the range of the data.
  • Correlation does not mean causation, and predictions outside the data range are unreliable.

Example 4.1 Organizing survey data

Twenty students reported their main mode of transport to RTU: J, J, M, B, J, T, J, M, J, B, L, J, T, J, M, J, B, J, L, J (J = jeepney, M = MRT, B = bus, L = LRT, T = tricycle). (a) Make a frequency table with relative frequencies. (b) What kind of data are these, and which graph is appropriate?

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Tally each category, divide each count by 20, and check that the frequencies add to 20 and the percents to 100%.

Solution:

(a) Jeepney 10 (50%), MRT 3 (15%), Bus 3 (15%), LRT 2 (10%), Tricycle 2 (10%). Total 20 (100%).

(b) Qualitative (categorical) data. A bar graph compares the categories; a pie chart is also fine because the categories are parts of one whole. The mode is jeepney.

Check:

\(10 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 2 = 20\) ✓ and \(50 + 15 + 15 + 10 + 10 = 100\%\) ✓. A histogram would be wrong: the categories have no numerical order.

Example 4.2 Mean, median, mode: which one?

Daily allowances (pesos) of 12 students: 100, 120, 150, 150, 150, 180, 200, 200, 250, 300, 350, 600. Find the mean, median and mode, and decide which best represents a typical allowance.

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

The data are already sorted. Compute each measure, then look for an outlier that might pull the mean.

Solution:

Mean: \(\sum x = 2750\), \(\bar{x} = 2750/12 \approx 229.17\) pesos.

Median: \(n = 12\), so average the 6th and 7th values: \((180 + 200)/2 = 190\) pesos.

Mode: 150 pesos (three times).

The allowance of 600 is far above the rest and pulls the mean up: 8 of the 12 students receive less than the mean. The data are right-skewed, so the median, 190 pesos, best represents a typical allowance.

Check:

Without the 600, the mean of the other 11 values is \(2150/11 \approx 195\), close to the median, confirming that one value was distorting the mean. Order: mode 150 < median 190 < mean 229, as expected for right skew.

Example 4.3 Standard deviation of the commute data

Find the range, the sample variance and the sample standard deviation of the commute times 15, 25, 30, 45, 50, 60, 75, 80, 90, 120 minutes, and interpret the result.

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Follow the steps: mean, deviations, squared deviations, sum, divide by \(n - 1\), square root.

Solution:

Range \(= 120 - 15 = 105\) min. Mean \(\bar{x} = 590/10 = 59\).

Squared deviations: 1936, 1156, 841, 196, 81, 1, 256, 441, 961, 3721; sum \(= 9590\).

\(s^2 = 9590/(10 - 1) \approx 1065.56\) min², and \(s = \sqrt{1065.56} \approx 32.64\) min.

Interpretation: commutes average 59 minutes and typically differ from that average by about 33 minutes, a large spread.

Check:

The deviations add to 0 ✓. \(32.64^2 \approx 1065.4\) ✓. As a rough check, \(s\) should be about range/4 for moderate data sets: \(105/4 \approx 26\), the same order of size.

Example 4.4 Where does one value stand?

In the commute data (\(\bar{x} = 59\), \(s = 32.64\)), find the z-score and the percentile rank of a 90-minute commute and of a 30-minute commute. Interpret.

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

\(z = (x - \bar{x})/s\); percentile rank \(=\) (number of values below \(x\)) / \(n \times 100\).

Solution:

90 min: \(z = 31/32.64 \approx 0.95\); 8 values are below 90, so percentile rank \(= 80\). It is about 0.95 standard deviations above average and longer than 80% of the commutes.

30 min: \(z = -29/32.64 \approx -0.89\); 2 values (15, 25) are below 30, so percentile rank \(= 20\). It is about 0.89 standard deviations below average and longer than only 20% of the commutes. (30 is also \(Q_1\).)

Check:

Signs make sense: above the mean gives positive \(z\), below gives negative. A higher value has a higher percentile rank ✓.

Example 4.5 A normal probability

Heights of male RTU freshmen are approximately normal with mean 168 cm and standard deviation 6 cm. Find the probability that a randomly chosen male freshman is (a) taller than 177 cm; (b) between 162 and 174 cm.

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Convert to z-scores, sketch and shade, then use the z-table (area to the left) and the empirical rule.

Solution:

(a) \(z = (177 - 168)/6 = 1.50\). Area left of 1.50 is 0.9332, so \(P(X > 177) = 1 - 0.9332 = 0.0668\), about 6.7%.

(b) 162 and 174 are one standard deviation below and above the mean (\(z = -1\) and \(z = 1\)). Area \(= 0.8413 - (1 - 0.8413) = 0.6826\), about 68%, matching the empirical rule.

Check:

(a) 177 cm is above average, so the probability should be well under 0.5 ✓. (b) The 68% of the empirical rule ✓.

Example 4.6 Correlation and regression

Using the distance–time data of Section 4.6 (\(n = 10\), \(\sum x = 150\), \(\sum y = 725\), \(\sum xy = 12\,700\), \(\sum x^2 = 2682\), \(\sum y^2 = 61\,475\)): find \(r\), the regression line, and the predicted commute of a student living 20 km from RTU. Is it reasonable to predict for 60 km?

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Compute the three building blocks \(n\sum xy - \sum x\sum y\), \(n\sum x^2 - (\sum x)^2\) and \(n\sum y^2 - (\sum y)^2\) once, then reuse them.

Solution:

\(S_{xy} = 127\,000 - 108\,750 = 18\,250\); \(S_{xx} = 26\,820 - 22\,500 = 4320\); \(S_{yy} = 614\,750 - 525\,625 = 89\,125\).

\(r = 18\,250/\sqrt{4320 \times 89\,125} \approx 0.93\) (very strong positive).

\(b = 18\,250/4320 \approx 4.2245\); \(a = 72.5 - 4.2245(15) \approx 9.13\). Line: \(\hat{y} = 9.13 + 4.22x\).

At \(x = 20\): \(\hat{y} \approx 9.13 + 84.49 \approx 93.6\) minutes, about an hour and a half.

60 km is far outside the data (4 to 25 km), so a prediction there would be extrapolation and is not reliable.

Check:

The line passes through \((\bar{x}, \bar{y}) = (15, 72.5)\): \(9.13 + 4.22(15) = 72.4\) ✓ (rounding). \(r^2 \approx 0.865\), consistent with the points lying close to the line in Figure 4.7.
