✎Problems

  1. (§3.1) For each sequence, use inductive reasoning to give the next two terms and describe the rule: (a) 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, … (b) 1, 2, 6, 24, 120, … (c) 80, 40, 20, 10, …

  2. (§3.1, explain) Find a counterexample to each statement, and explain why one counterexample is enough: (a) For every real number \(x\), \(x^2 > x\). (b) The sum of any two prime numbers is even. (c) If a number is divisible by 4, it is divisible by 8.

  3. (§3.1) Classify each argument as inductive or deductive, and state its conclusion: (a) All triangles have angles that add to 180°; the corner of this sail is a triangle, so its angles add to 180°. (b) The jeepney to Pasig came every 10 minutes all week, so it will come every 10 minutes tomorrow. (c) Every multiple of 6 is even; 4206 is a multiple of 6.

  4. (§3.2, prove) (a) Prove that the product of two odd numbers is odd. (b) Prove that the sum of three consecutive integers is always divisible by 3.

  5. (§3.2) Use Gauss's method to find (a) \(1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots + 500\); (b) the sum of the even numbers \(2 + 4 + 6 + \cdots + 200\); (c) \(51 + 52 + \cdots + 150\).

  6. (§3.3) Solve using Pólya's four steps, writing each step: A print shop charges 2 pesos per page for the first 50 pages and 1.50 pesos per page after that. (a) How much does it cost to print a 130-page thesis? (b) A student paid 175 pesos. How many pages were printed?

  7. (§3.4) A coin purse has 20 coins, all 5-peso and 10-peso coins, worth 145 pesos in all. How many of each coin are there? Solve once by guess and check (show your table of guesses) and once with an equation.

  8. (§3.4) Work backwards: Carla thought of a number, multiplied it by 3, subtracted 7, divided the result by 2, and got 19. What was her number? Check your answer.

  9. (§3.4) Solve a simpler problem first: how many diagonals does a polygon with 12 sides have? Make a table for 4, 5, 6 and 7 sides and find the pattern.

  10. (§3.5) For the arithmetic sequence 7, 11, 15, 19, …: (a) find a formula for \(a_n\); (b) find \(a_{20}\); (c) find the sum of the first 20 terms. Then, for the geometric sequence 2, 6, 18, 54, …, find \(a_8\).

  11. (§3.5) Use a difference table to find the next term and a formula for the \(n\)th term of 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, … Then find the 10th term.

  12. (§3.6) (a) How many moves are needed to solve the Tower of Hanoi with 8 disks? At one move per second, how many minutes is that? (b) Fill in a 3 × 3 magic square using the numbers 2 to 10 (each once). What must each line add to, and what number must go in the centre? Explain.

  13. (§4.1–4.3) The daily allowances (pesos) of 12 students are 100, 120, 150, 150, 150, 180, 200, 200, 250, 300, 350, 600. (a) Classify the data. (b) Find the mean, median and mode. (c) Find the range and the sample standard deviation. (d) Which measures of centre and spread would you report, and why?

  14. (§4.4) For the allowances in Problem 13: (a) find \(Q_1\), \(Q_3\) and the IQR (course method); (b) draw a box plot; (c) use the \(1.5 \times \text{IQR}\) rule to decide whether 600 is an outlier; (d) find the z-score and the percentile rank of 300.

  15. (§4.5) Heights of male RTU freshmen are approximately normal with mean 168 cm and standard deviation 6 cm. Find the probability that a randomly chosen male freshman is (a) taller than 180 cm; (b) shorter than 159 cm; (c) between 162 and 174 cm. Sketch and shade the curve each time.

  16. (§4.6) Six students recorded hours of study \(x\) and quiz score \(y\): (1, 55), (2, 60), (3, 68), (4, 70), (5, 78), (6, 85). (a) Draw a scatterplot. (b) Compute Pearson's \(r\) and describe the relationship. (c) Find the least-squares line. (d) Predict the score of a student who studied 4.5 hours. (e) Explain why the line should not be used to predict the score for 15 hours of study, and why this data alone does not prove that studying causes higher scores.
