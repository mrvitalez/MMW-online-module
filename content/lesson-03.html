3.1Inductive and Deductive Reasoning

Look at the list 2, 4, 8, 16, … and you will almost certainly say the next number is 32. You did not prove it; you noticed that every number so far is double the one before and assumed the habit continues. That habit of mind is called inductive reasoning, and it is one of the two great ways people reach conclusions. The other, deductive reasoning, runs in the opposite direction. Mathematics uses both, but it trusts them differently.

Definition Inductive reasoning is the process of reaching a general conclusion by examining specific examples. The conclusion is called a conjecture: an idea based on incomplete information, which may be true or false.

Induction is how science, weather forecasting and everyday life work. A PAGASA forecaster who has seen hundreds of storms behave a certain way expects the next one to behave similarly. A commuter who has waited 20 minutes for a jeepney at 7:00 a.m. for a whole month expects the same tomorrow. These conclusions are probable, not guaranteed. The more cases you check, and the more varied they are, the stronger the conjecture becomes, but no number of cases makes it certain.

Example Compute \(1 = 1\), \(1 + 3 = 4\), \(1 + 3 + 5 = 9\), \(1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16\). The sums are 1, 4, 9, 16, the perfect squares. Conjecture: the sum of the first \(n\) odd numbers is \(n^2\). (This one is true, and Section 3.2 shows how to prove it.)

Counterexamples. A conjecture that says "always" can be destroyed by a single case where it fails. Such a case is a counterexample. The statement "every odd number is prime" survives 3, 5 and 7, but 9 = 3 × 3 is a counterexample, so the statement is false. One counterexample is enough to disprove a general claim; a thousand examples are not enough to prove one.

Definition Deductive reasoning is the process of reaching a specific conclusion by applying general assumptions, procedures or principles (definitions, rules, theorems) that are accepted as true. If the premises are true and the reasoning is valid, the conclusion must be true.

Deduction moves from the general to the specific. "All members of the club can use the club's pool. Deanna is a member. Therefore Deanna can use the pool." Or, in mathematics: "A number is divisible by 3 if the sum of its digits is divisible by 3. The digits of 339 add up to 15, which is divisible by 3. Therefore 339 is divisible by 3." Solving a Sudoku is deduction too: every number you write is forced by the rules and the clues already on the grid.

Common mistake Treating a pattern that worked for a few cases as proven. The expression \(n^2 + n + 41\) gives a prime number for \(n = 0, 1, 2, \dots, 39\), forty cases in a row, yet for \(n = 40\) it gives \(1681 = 41^2\). Checking many cases is evidence, not proof.
Common mistake Thinking deduction is always right. "All engineering students are good at drawing; Paolo is an engineering student; so Paolo is good at drawing" is valid reasoning, but the first premise is false, so the conclusion is not guaranteed.

3.2Intuition, Proof, and Certainty

A famous story tells of young Carl Friedrich Gauss, whose teacher asked the class to add the whole numbers from 1 to 100 to keep them busy. Gauss answered almost at once: 5050. He did not add one by one. He noticed that \(1 + 100 = 101\), \(2 + 99 = 101\), \(3 + 98 = 101\), and so on: the numbers pair up into 50 pairs, each adding to 101, so the total is \(50 \times 101 = 5050\).

That flash of insight is intuition. Notice what happened next, though: the pairing idea can be turned into an argument that works for any \(n\), not just 100. That argument is a proof, and it gives certainty.

Definition Intuition is an immediate sense or idea about what might be true; it suggests an answer. A proof is a logical argument showing that a mathematical statement must be true under stated assumptions; it establishes the answer. Certainty is the degree to which we can justify that a conclusion necessarily follows.

From 100 to every \(n\). Write the sum \(S = 1 + 2 + \cdots + n\) forwards and backwards and add the two lines term by term. Each column adds to \(n + 1\), and there are \(n\) columns, so \(2S = n(n + 1)\).

Key equations
\[ 1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} \]
\[ 1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + (2n - 1) = n^2 \]

A direct proof. To prove a statement about all numbers of a kind, represent a general member with a variable. An even number is any number of the form \(2k\), and an odd number is any number of the form \(2k + 1\), where \(k\) is an integer.

Example Claim: the sum of two odd numbers is even. Proof: Let the odd numbers be \(2a + 1\) and \(2b + 1\) for integers \(a, b\). Their sum is \(2a + 2b + 2 = 2(a + b + 1)\). Since \(a + b + 1\) is an integer, the sum has the form \(2k\), so it is even. ∎
Notice that we did not test examples. The letters \(a\) and \(b\) stand for every possible pair, so the argument covers infinitely many cases at once.

Where intuition helps and where it fails. Intuition is the engine of discovery: almost every theorem began as somebody's hunch. But intuition can mislead. Most people guess that in a room of 23 people a shared birthday is unlikely; in fact the probability is just over 50%. Most people guess that folding a sheet of paper 42 times gives a stack a few metres high; in fact it would reach past the Moon. In mathematics a hunch becomes knowledge only when it is proved.

Degrees of certainty. A result checked in a few cases is a conjecture. A result supported by huge amounts of evidence (Goldbach's conjecture that every even number greater than 2 is a sum of two primes has been checked beyond \(4 \times 10^{18}\)) is very likely, but still not proven. A proved theorem, such as "there are infinitely many primes", is certain within the rules of mathematics, and will stay true forever. This permanence is what sets mathematical knowledge apart from scientific knowledge, which is always open to revision by new observations.

Common mistake "Proving" a general statement with an example: "\(3 + 5 = 8\) is even, so the sum of two odd numbers is even." An example only illustrates; a proof must work for every case, which is why we use variables.

3.3Pólya's Four Steps in Problem Solving

Many students freeze in front of a word problem, not because the mathematics is hard but because they do not know where to start. The Hungarian mathematician George Pólya (1887–1985) studied how good problem solvers think and described their method in four steps in his book How to Solve It (1945). The steps are simple to state and powerful to practise.

  1. Understand the problem. Read it more than once. What is given? What is asked? What are the conditions? Restate the problem in your own words, draw a picture, introduce notation, and estimate what a sensible answer looks like.
  2. Devise a plan. Decide how to connect the given to the asked. Have you seen a similar problem? Which strategy fits: a table, a diagram, an equation, working backwards, looking for a pattern (Section 3.4)?
  3. Carry out the plan. Do the work carefully, checking each step. If the plan does not work, go back and choose another. Write a complete, readable solution and box your final answer.
  4. Look back. Does the answer make sense? Does it satisfy every condition of the problem? Can you check it another way? Could the method solve other problems?

You may know the same idea under the names GAFSA (Given, Asked, Formula, Solution, Answer) or GRESA (Given, Required, Equation, Solution, Answer). "Given" and "Asked/Required" are step 1, "Formula/Equation" is step 2, "Solution" is step 3, and a boxed "Answer" that you check is step 4.

Example A printing problem. A shop near RTU charges 3 pesos per page for the first 20 pages and 2 pesos per page after that. How much does a 55-page report cost?
Understand: Given: 3 pesos/page for pages 1–20, 2 pesos/page beyond; 55 pages. Asked: the total cost. Estimate: between \(55 \times 2 = 110\) and \(55 \times 3 = 165\) pesos.
Plan: Split the job into the first 20 pages and the remaining pages.
Carry out: \(20 \times 3 = 60\); \(55 - 20 = 35\) pages at 2 pesos is \(70\); total \(60 + 70 = 130\) pesos.
Look back: 130 lies in the estimated range. Check: average cost \(130/55 \approx 2.36\) pesos per page, between 2 and 3, as expected.

Why "look back" matters. It is the step students skip most, and the one that catches the most errors. Ask: are the units right? Is the size reasonable (a jeepney fare of 2,600 pesos is not)? If the problem says "how many people", is the answer a whole number? Substituting your answer back into the original conditions is the fastest check.

Common mistake Jumping to step 3: grabbing the numbers in the problem and combining them with whatever operation "looks right". If you cannot say in words what is given and what is asked, you are not ready to compute.

3.4Problem-Solving Strategies

Step 2 of Pólya's method asks you to devise a plan. Experienced solvers carry a toolbox of strategies. None works every time, but knowing several lets you try another when one fails. Here are the most useful ones, each with a quick illustration.

StrategyWhen it helps
Guess and check (guess, test, revise)few possible answers; easy to test a guess
Make a table or an organized listcounting cases, finding a pattern
Draw a diagrampositions, distances, arrangements, sets
Work backwardsthe final result is known, the start is not
Look for a patternsequences, repeated processes
Solve a simpler problemlarge numbers; try small cases first
Write an equationrelationships between unknown quantities

Guess and check. A farmer has chickens and pigs: 30 heads and 84 legs. Guess 15 of each: legs \(= 30 + 60 = 90\), too many. Fewer pigs: 12 pigs and 18 chickens give \(48 + 36 = 84\). ✓ Organized guessing (each guess informed by the last) quickly homes in.

Write an equation. The same problem: let \(p\) = pigs, so chickens \(= 30 - p\) and \(4p + 2(30 - p) = 84\), giving \(2p = 24\), \(p = 12\). The equation is guess and check done once, in general.

Work backwards. "Bea spent half her allowance on lunch, then 20 pesos on a drink, and has 35 pesos left. How much did she start with?" Undo each step in reverse order: before the drink she had \(35 + 20 = 55\); before lunch she had \(2 \times 55 = 110\) pesos.

Solve a simpler problem, make a table, look for a pattern. How many handshakes happen if each of 10 people shakes hands with everyone else once? Start small: 2 people, 1 handshake; 3 people, 3; 4 people, 6; 5 people, 10. A table shows that adding the \(n\)th person adds \(n - 1\) new handshakes, so the totals are \(1, 3, 6, 10, 15, \dots\): the triangular numbers. For \(n\) people there are \(\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\) handshakes, so 10 people make 45.

Draw a diagram. A snail at the bottom of a 10-metre well climbs 3 m each day and slides back 2 m each night. When does it get out? A sketch shows the trap: it gains 1 m per full day, but on the day it starts at 7 m it climbs to 10 m and is out before sliding. So it escapes on day 8, not day 10.

Common mistake Stopping at the first strategy that fails. If guess and check is slow, switch to an equation; if the numbers are too big, solve a smaller version first. Changing strategy is part of the plan, not a sign of failure.

3.5Mathematical Problems Involving Patterns

Chapter 1 showed patterns in nature; here we learn to describe number patterns exactly so we can predict any term. A sequence is an ordered list of numbers \(a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots\), called terms. The goal is usually a formula for the \(n\)th term \(a_n\).

Definition An arithmetic sequence adds the same number \(d\) (the common difference) each time: 5, 9, 13, 17, … has \(d = 4\). A geometric sequence multiplies by the same number \(r\) (the common ratio) each time: 3, 6, 12, 24, … has \(r = 2\).
Key equations
\[ a_n = a_1 + (n - 1)d, \qquad S_n = \frac{n(a_1 + a_n)}{2} \]
\[ a_n = a_1 r^{\,n-1} \]
Example For 5, 9, 13, 17, …: \(a_n = 5 + (n - 1)4 = 4n + 1\). The 50th term is \(4(50) + 1 = 201\). For 3, 6, 12, …: \(a_n = 3 \cdot 2^{\,n-1}\), so \(a_{10} = 3 \cdot 2^9 = 1536\).

Difference tables. When a sequence is neither arithmetic nor geometric, list the differences between consecutive terms, then the differences of those differences. If the first differences are constant, the \(n\)th term is linear (\(an + b\)). If the second differences are constant, it is quadratic (\(an^2 + bn + c\)). The table also lets you extend the sequence: work from the bottom row upward.

The second differences are constant (2), so the next first difference is 12 and the next term is \(30 + 12 = 42\). In fact \(a_n = n(n + 1)\): \(1 \cdot 2, 2 \cdot 3, 3 \cdot 4, \dots\)

Figurate numbers. Some sequences count dots arranged in shapes. The triangular numbers 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, … satisfy \(T_n = \dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\), the Gauss sum again. The square numbers 1, 4, 9, 16, … are \(S_n = n^2\). Two consecutive triangular numbers always make a square: \(T_3 + T_4 = 6 + 10 = 16\).

Common mistake Writing \(a_n = a_1 + nd\) instead of \(a_1 + (n - 1)d\). Check with \(n = 1\): the formula must give back the first term. For 5, 9, 13, …, \(5 + 4n\) gives 9 at \(n = 1\), which is wrong.

3.6Recreational Problems Using Mathematics

Puzzles and games have been a playground for mathematics for centuries. They are worth your time because they train exactly the skills of this chapter: reading conditions carefully, reasoning deductively, organizing cases and spotting patterns, all without a formula sheet.

Magic squares. In a 3 × 3 magic square the numbers 1 to 9 are placed so that every row, column and diagonal has the same sum. Since \(1 + 2 + \cdots + 9 = 45\) is shared by three rows, each line must add to \(45 / 3 = 15\). Further reasoning shows the centre must be 5: the four lines through the centre use every number once plus the centre three extra times, so \(4 \times 15 = 45 + 3c\), giving \(c = 5\). Deduction, not trial and error, solves the puzzle.

The Tower of Hanoi. Move a stack of \(n\) disks from peg A to peg C, one disk at a time, never placing a larger disk on a smaller one. With 1 disk: 1 move. With 2 disks: 3 moves. With 3 disks: 7 moves. To move \(n\) disks you must move the top \(n - 1\) out of the way, move the largest disk, then move the \(n - 1\) back on top, so \(M_n = 2M_{n-1} + 1\). The pattern 1, 3, 7, 15, 31, … is \(M_n = 2^n - 1\). A legend says monks are moving 64 golden disks; at one move per second they need \(2^{64} - 1\) seconds, about 585 billion years.

Logic grid puzzles and Sudoku. In a Sudoku each row, column and 3 × 3 box contains 1 to 9 exactly once. Every correct entry is forced by deduction: "this cell cannot be 1, 2, 4, … because those already appear in its row, column or box, so it must be 7." KenKen adds arithmetic: cages of cells must produce a target with a given operation.

River crossing. A farmer must ferry a wolf, a goat and a cabbage across a river in a boat that holds only the farmer and one item. Left alone, the wolf eats the goat and the goat eats the cabbage. A diagram of states (who is on which bank) shows the solution in 7 crossings: take the goat over; return; take the wolf; bring the goat back; take the cabbage; return; take the goat. The key insight, carrying something backwards, comes from listing the allowed states systematically.

Age and coin puzzles are disguised equations. "Lola is four times as old as Ben; in 20 years she will be twice as old as he will be." Let Ben be \(b\): \(4b + 20 = 2(b + 20)\) so \(b = 10\) and Lola is 40. "I have 20 coins, all 5-peso and 10-peso coins, worth 145 pesos" gives \(x + y = 20\) and \(5x + 10y = 145\), so there are nine 10-peso coins and eleven 5-peso coins.

Common mistake Answering a puzzle by trying moves at random and stopping at the first answer that seems to work. Always verify every condition (for the river crossing, check each bank after every trip) and ask whether a shorter solution exists.

★Key takeaways

  • Inductive reasoning goes from specific cases to a general conjecture; its conclusions are probable, never certain.
  • Deductive reasoning applies accepted general rules to reach a specific conclusion that must be true if the premises are true.
  • One counterexample disproves a general claim; no number of examples proves one.
  • Intuition suggests, proof establishes: a proof uses variables so that it covers every case at once.
  • Pólya's four steps are understand the problem, devise a plan, carry out the plan, and look back.
  • Keep several strategies ready (table, diagram, work backwards, simpler problem, pattern, equation) and switch when one stalls.
  • Arithmetic sequences: \(a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d\); geometric: \(a_n = a_1 r^{n-1}\); constant second differences mean a quadratic pattern.
  • Puzzles such as magic squares and the Tower of Hanoi (\(2^n - 1\) moves) are solved by deduction and pattern finding, not luck.

Example 3.1 A pattern that breaks

Evaluate \(p(n) = n^2 + n + 41\) for \(n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\) and decide whether each value is prime. Make a conjecture, then test it at \(n = 40\). What does this show about inductive reasoning?

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Compute the first few cases (induction), state a conjecture, then look for a counterexample.

Solution:

\(p(1) = 43\), \(p(2) = 47\), \(p(3) = 53\), \(p(4) = 61\), \(p(5) = 71\); all are prime. Conjecture: \(n^2 + n + 41\) is prime for every positive integer \(n\).

At \(n = 40\): \(p(40) = 1600 + 40 + 41 = 1681 = 41 \times 41\), which is not prime. So \(n = 40\) is a counterexample and the conjecture is false.

Inductive reasoning gave a reasonable conjecture, supported by many cases (in fact every \(n\) from 0 to 39 works), but it was still wrong. Only a proof gives certainty; one counterexample is enough to disprove.

Check:

\(41^2 = 1681\) ✓. You can also see it without computing: \(p(40) = 40 \cdot 41 + 41 = 41(40 + 1) = 41^2\).

Example 3.2 Proving a sum formula

(a) Find \(1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots + 250\). (b) Prove that the sum of an even number and an odd number is odd.

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

(a) Use Gauss's pairing formula \(n(n+1)/2\). (b) Represent a general even number as \(2a\) and a general odd number as \(2b + 1\).

Solution:

(a) With \(n = 250\): \(\dfrac{250 \times 251}{2} = 125 \times 251 = 31\,375\).

(b) Let the even number be \(2a\) and the odd number be \(2b + 1\), with \(a, b\) integers. Their sum is \(2a + 2b + 1 = 2(a + b) + 1\). Since \(a + b\) is an integer, the sum has the form \(2k + 1\), so it is odd. ∎

Check:

(a) Pairing: 125 pairs \((1 + 250, 2 + 249, \dots)\), each 251: \(125 \times 251 = 31\,375\) ✓. (b) Try one case to see the claim is plausible: \(8 + 5 = 13\), odd ✓ (but the proof, not the example, is what establishes it).

Example 3.3 Pólya's steps: a jeepney fare

A traditional jeepney charges 13 pesos for the first 4 km and 1.80 pesos for each additional kilometre. Mara rides 11 km to RTU. How much is her fare? Use Pólya's four steps.

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Separate the base distance from the extra distance, the same way the fare rule does.

Solution:

Understand. Given: 13 pesos covers 4 km; each km beyond costs 1.80; trip = 11 km. Asked: fare. Estimate: a bit more than 13 pesos, much less than \(11 \times 13\).

Plan. Extra distance \(= 11 - 4\); fare \(= 13 + 1.80 \times\) extra.

Carry out. Extra distance \(= 7\) km; \(1.80 \times 7 = 12.60\); fare \(= 13 + 12.60 = 25.60\) pesos.

Look back. For exactly 4 km the formula gives 13 pesos, matching the rule. 25.60 pesos is a reasonable fare for 11 km.

Check:

Alternative: the fare for 11 km is the fare for 4 km plus 7 steps of 1.80: \(13 + 1.8 + 1.8 + \cdots\) (7 times) \(= 25.60\) ✓.

Example 3.4 Handshakes: a simpler problem first

At a block meeting, each of the 25 students shakes hands with every other student exactly once. How many handshakes are there?

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

25 people is too many to list. Solve simpler problems (2, 3, 4, 5 people), make a table, look for a pattern, then generalize.

Solution:

Small cases: 2 people → 1; 3 → 3; 4 → 6; 5 → 10. Each new person shakes hands with everyone already there, so the \(n\)th person adds \(n - 1\) handshakes: totals \(1, 1 + 2, 1 + 2 + 3, \dots\)

For \(n\) people the total is \(1 + 2 + \cdots + (n - 1) = \dfrac{(n - 1)n}{2}\). With \(n = 25\): \(\dfrac{24 \times 25}{2} = 300\) handshakes.

Check:

Another view: each of 25 people shakes 24 hands, giving \(25 \times 24 = 600\), but this counts every handshake twice (once for each person), so \(600 / 2 = 300\) ✓. The formula also gives 10 for 5 people, matching the table.

Example 3.5 An auditorium pattern

The first row of an auditorium has 20 seats, and each row has 3 more seats than the row in front of it. There are 15 rows. (a) How many seats are in the 15th row? (b) How many seats are there in all?

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

The seat counts 20, 23, 26, … form an arithmetic sequence with \(a_1 = 20\), \(d = 3\). Use \(a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d\) and \(S_n = n(a_1 + a_n)/2\).

Solution:

(a) \(a_{15} = 20 + (15 - 1)(3) = 20 + 42 = 62\) seats.

(b) \(S_{15} = \dfrac{15(20 + 62)}{2} = \dfrac{15 \times 82}{2} = 615\) seats.

Check:

Average row size \(= (20 + 62)/2 = 41\); \(15 \times 41 = 615\) ✓. The answer for (a) is a whole number of seats, as it must be.

Example 3.6 An age puzzle

Lola Ising is now 4 times as old as her grandson Ben. In 20 years she will be only twice as old as he will be then. How old are they now?

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Write an equation. Let Ben's present age be \(b\); express both ages now and in 20 years.

Solution:

Now: Ben \(b\), Lola \(4b\). In 20 years: Ben \(b + 20\), Lola \(4b + 20\). The condition gives \(4b + 20 = 2(b + 20)\), so \(4b + 20 = 2b + 40\), \(2b = 20\), \(b = 10\).

Ben is 10 and Lola Ising is 40.

Check:

Now: \(40 = 4 \times 10\) ✓. In 20 years: Lola 60, Ben 30, and \(60 = 2 \times 30\) ✓.
