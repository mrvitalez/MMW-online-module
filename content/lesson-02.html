2.1Characteristics of Mathematical Language: Precise, Concise, Powerful

Mathematics is often called a language: it has its own vocabulary (symbols such as \(+\), \(=\), \(\in\), \(\forall\)), its own grammar (rules for combining symbols correctly), and its own literature (definitions, theorems and proofs). Like any language, it was developed to communicate, but it is built to say certain kinds of things far better than everyday speech. Its three defining characteristics are that it is precise, concise and powerful.

Definition The language of mathematics is
  • precise: it can make very fine distinctions, and each statement has one specific meaning;
  • concise: it can say things briefly, using symbols to communicate complicated ideas economically;
  • powerful: it can express complex thoughts with relative ease, and once written in symbols an idea can be manipulated and generalized.

Precise. Consider the everyday sentence "There is at least one student in the class who is taller than 180 cm." Questions arise at once: who exactly? What does "at least one" mean? Is 180 cm itself included? Ordinary language leaves these open; mathematics tries to give each statement one specific meaning. We rewrite it as: "There exists a student \(x\) in the class \(S\) whose height \(h(x)\) is greater than 180 cm." Now "at least one" means "there exists", the set of people is named, and "taller than" means strictly \(>\), so a student who is exactly 180 cm does not count. Compare it with the vague "There is at least one tall student", which depends on what each listener thinks "tall" means.

Concise. The same idea in symbols is \[ \exists\, x \in S \ \big(h(x) > 180\big). \] Seven symbols replace a long sentence (Figure 2.1). Another example: "the square of the sum of two numbers is equal to the sum of their squares plus twice their product" is simply \((a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2\).

Powerful. Once an idea is in symbols, we can change it with small, exact edits instead of rewriting paragraphs:

  • "Everyone is taller than 180 cm": \(\forall x \in S\ \big(h(x) > 180\big)\).
  • "Nobody is taller than 180 cm": \(\forall x \in S\ \big(h(x) \le 180\big)\), which is the negation of the original.
  • "At least one student is taller than 180 cm and weighs more than 80 kg": \(\exists x \in S\ \big(h(x) > 180 \wedge w(x) > 80\big)\).

Symbols also let us calculate with ideas: the rule \((a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2\) works for every pair of numbers at once, so \(103^2 = (100 + 3)^2 = 10\,000 + 600 + 9 = 10\,609\) can be done mentally.

Common mistake Mathematical words can have meanings different from everyday words. In mathematics "or" is usually inclusive (one or the other or both), "some" means "at least one" (possibly all), and "a number is positive" excludes 0. Read definitions literally.

2.2Expressions versus Sentences

In English we distinguish nouns (which name things) from sentences (which state complete thoughts). Mathematics has the same distinction: an expression is the mathematical analogue of a noun, and a mathematical sentence is the analogue of a sentence.

Definition A mathematical expression is a name given to a mathematical object of interest (usually a number, a set, a function). It does not assert anything, so it is neither true nor false. A mathematical sentence makes an assertion (a complete thought) and therefore can have a truth value.

Examples. \(5 + 3\), \(\frac{x}{2}\), \(3x^2 - 7\), \(\{1, 2, 3\}\), \(\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\) are expressions. Asking "Is \(3x^2 - 7\) true?" makes no sense, just as "Is the red jeepney true?" makes no sense. In contrast, \(5 + 3 = 8\), \(x > 2\), \(2 \in \{1, 2, 3\}\) and "every even number greater than 2 is the sum of two primes" are sentences. The verbs of mathematics are the symbols that assert: \(=\), \(\ne\), \(<\), \(\le\), \(>\), \(\ge\), \(\in\), \(\subseteq\), and words like "is" or "divides".

Open and closed sentences. Sentences come in two types:

  • A closed sentence has no missing parts (no free variables) and is clearly true or false: \(3 + 4 = 7\) (true), \(10 \div 2 = 4\) (false), \(7 < 9\) (true). Closed sentences use only numbers and symbols.
  • An open sentence contains a variable such as \(x\), \(y\) or \(n\), so it is neither true nor false until the variable is replaced or quantified: \(x + 5 = 9\) is true when \(x = 4\) and false otherwise. The values that make it true form its solution set.
Example "\(2x + 1\)" is an expression. "\(2x + 1 = 7\)" is an open sentence; replacing \(x\) by 3 gives the true closed sentence \(2(3) + 1 = 7\), and replacing \(x\) by 5 gives the false closed sentence \(2(5) + 1 = 7\).
ItemTypeTruth value

Common mistake "Solving" an expression is meaningless; you simplify or evaluate an expression, and you solve an open sentence (an equation or inequality). Writing "\(= 22\)" after an expression turns it into a sentence that must then be true.

2.3Conventions in the Mathematical Language

Every language has conventions: agreed habits that are not logically forced but that everyone follows so that writing is understood the same way. Mathematics is read by people all over the world, so its conventions matter.

Conventions about symbols.

  • Letters at the end of the alphabet (\(x, y, z\)) usually stand for variables (unknowns); letters at the beginning (\(a, b, c\)) usually stand for constants; \(n, m, k\) usually stand for whole numbers (integers); \(f, g, h\) name functions.
  • Capital letters (\(A, B, S, U\)) name sets; lowercase letters name their elements.
  • Special sets have fixed names: \(\mathbb{N}\) (natural numbers 1, 2, 3, …), \(\mathbb{Z}\) (integers), \(\mathbb{Q}\) (rational numbers), \(\mathbb{R}\) (real numbers).
  • Greek letters have common roles: \(\pi \approx 3.14159\) is a fixed constant, \(\Sigma\) means "sum", \(\theta\) often names an angle, \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) name the mean and standard deviation in statistics (Module 4).
  • Multiplication is often written by juxtaposition: \(3x\) means \(3 \times x\) and \(ab\) means \(a \times b\); but \(23\) is twenty-three, not \(2 \times 3\).

The order of operations. To give an expression like \(3 + 4 \times 2^2\) a single value, everyone agrees to evaluate in this order: (1) grouping symbols (parentheses, brackets, fraction bars, radicals), (2) exponents, (3) multiplication and division from left to right, (4) addition and subtraction from left to right. This is remembered in the Philippines as PEMDAS or GEMDAS. So \(3 + 4 \times 2^2 = 3 + 4 \times 4 = 3 + 16 = 19\), not \(7 \times 4 = 28\).

Example \(18 \div 3 \times 2 + 4^2 - (5 - 2)^2 = 18 \div 3 \times 2 + 16 - 9 = 6 \times 2 + 16 - 9 = 12 + 16 - 9 = 19\). Division and multiplication have equal rank, so we go left to right: \(18 \div 3 = 6\) first, then \(\times 2\).
Common mistake "M before D" in PEMDAS does not mean multiplication always comes first. \(12 \div 2 \times 3 = 6 \times 3 = 18\), not \(12 \div 6 = 2\). Also, \(-3^2 = -9\) (the exponent applies to 3 only), while \((-3)^2 = 9\).

Conventions of writing. Mathematical writing uses complete sentences in which symbols play the role of words: "Since \(x > 2\), we have \(x^2 > 4\)." Keep the equal sign for things that are really equal: writing \(3 + 4 = 7 \times 2 = 14\) is a common error, because \(3 + 4\) is not 14. Name what your letters mean before using them ("Let \(t\) be the travel time in minutes"). Include units in applied problems.

2.4Four Basic Concepts: Sets, Functions, Relations, Binary Operations

Suppose you have a class of students. Three natural questions lead to the four basic concepts of mathematics: What objects are we talking about? (all the students: a set). Can we assign something to each student? (each student has exactly one student number: a function). How can two students be connected? ("Student A is taller than Student B": a relation). Finally, how do we combine two objects to get another? (a binary operation).

1. Sets.

Definition A set is a well-defined collection of objects, called its elements or members. "Well-defined" means we can always decide whether a given object belongs to it. We write \(a \in A\) for "\(a\) is an element of \(A\)" and \(a \notin A\) for "\(a\) is not an element of \(A\)".

A set can be written in roster form, \(A = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}\), or in set-builder form, \(A = \{x \in \mathbb{N} \mid x \le 5\}\), read "the set of all natural numbers \(x\) such that \(x\) is less than or equal to 5". Then \(2 \in A\) and \(7 \notin A\). "The set of tall students" is not a set in the mathematical sense, because "tall" is not well-defined.

Basic vocabulary: the empty set \(\varnothing = \{\}\) has no elements; \(n(A)\) is the number of elements (the cardinality); \(A \subseteq B\) (subset) means every element of \(A\) is in \(B\); the universal set \(U\) contains every object under discussion. A set with \(n\) elements has \(2^n\) subsets. The main operations (Figure 2.2):

  • union \(A \cup B = \{x \mid x \in A \text{ or } x \in B\}\);
  • intersection \(A \cap B = \{x \mid x \in A \text{ and } x \in B\}\);
  • difference \(A - B = \{x \mid x \in A \text{ and } x \notin B\}\);
  • complement \(A' = \{x \in U \mid x \notin A\}\).

For counting, \(n(A \cup B) = n(A) + n(B) - n(A \cap B)\): elements in both sets would otherwise be counted twice.

2. Relations. An ordered pair \((a, b)\) is a pair of objects written in a specific order, so \((2, 5) \ne (5, 2)\): if \((2, 5)\) means "student 2 is taller than student 5", then \((5, 2)\) says the opposite. The Cartesian product \(A \times B\) is the set of all ordered pairs \((a, b)\) with \(a \in A\), \(b \in B\).

Definition A relation from \(A\) to \(B\) is a set of ordered pairs, that is, a subset of \(A \times B\). It tells us whether, and how, two objects are connected. Its domain is the set of first elements and its range is the set of second elements.

For example, \(R = \{(1, 2), (1, 3), (2, 4)\}\) says "1 is related to 2", "1 is related to 3", "2 is related to 4", and "2 is not related to 3". Domain \(\{1, 2\}\), range \(\{2, 3, 4\}\). Relations can have properties: reflexive (every \(a\) is related to itself, like "\(=\)"), symmetric (if \(a\) is related to \(b\) then \(b\) is related to \(a\), like "is a classmate of"), and transitive (if \(a \sim b\) and \(b \sim c\) then \(a \sim c\), like "is taller than"). A relation with all three is an equivalence relation.

3. Functions.

Definition A function \(f\) from \(X\) to \(Y\) is a relation in which every element of \(X\) is paired with exactly one element of \(Y\). We write \(f: X \to Y\) and \(y = f(x)\). For every input there must be exactly one output.

Many inputs may share one output: that is still a function (many students can be in the same block). What is forbidden is one input with two outputs (one student with two student numbers). Figure 2.3 shows the difference. As a set of pairs, a relation is a function when no first element repeats: \(\{(1, a), (2, a), (3, b)\}\) is a function; \(\{(1, a), (1, b), (3, c)\}\) is not. For graphs in the \(xy\)-plane this is the vertical line test. A function given by a formula, such as \(f(x) = 3x^2 - 2x + 1\), is evaluated by substitution: \(f(-2) = 3(4) - 2(-2) + 1 = 17\).

4. Binary operations.

Definition A binary operation \(*\) on a set \(A\) is a rule that takes any two elements \(a, b \in A\) and produces exactly one element \(a * b\) that is also in \(A\). (The result stays inside the set: the set is closed under the operation.)

Addition is a binary operation on \(\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}\): the sum of two natural numbers is always a natural number. Subtraction is not a binary operation on \(\mathbb{N}\): \(5 - 3 = 2 \in \mathbb{N}\), but \(3 - 5 = -2 \notin \mathbb{N}\). Division is not a binary operation on \(\mathbb{Z}\) (\(1 \div 2\) is not an integer, and \(1 \div 0\) is undefined). Operations can also be defined by a rule, for example \(a * b = a + b - ab\) on \(\mathbb{Z}\): \(3 * (-2) = 3 - 2 + 6 = 7\).

2.5Elementary Logic: Connectives, Quantifiers, Negation, Variables

Mathematical sentences are combined and modified with a small number of logical words. Learning to use them exactly is the grammar of mathematics. (Module 5 develops logic in full; here we learn the vocabulary.)

Definition A proposition (or statement) is a sentence that is either true or false, but not both. Propositions are named by letters \(p, q, r, \dots\)

Connectives build compound statements from simple ones:

ConnectiveSymbolRead asTrue when
negation\(\neg p\) (or \(\sim p\))not \(p\)\(p\) is false
conjunction\(p \wedge q\)\(p\) and \(q\)both are true
disjunction\(p \vee q\)\(p\) or \(q\)at least one is true
conditional\(p \rightarrow q\)if \(p\), then \(q\)except when \(p\) is true and \(q\) is false
biconditional\(p \leftrightarrow q\)\(p\) if and only if \(q\)both have the same truth value

For example, with \(p\): "it is raining" and \(q\): "classes are suspended", \(p \wedge \neg q\) says "it is raining and classes are not suspended".

Variables. A variable is a symbol that stands for any element of a given set (its domain). Variables let us state general facts: "for every real number \(x\), \(x^2 \ge 0\)". A sentence with a free variable, like \(x > 3\), is open; it becomes a proposition once the variable is given a value or is bound by a quantifier.

Quantifiers say "how many" values of the variable make the sentence true:

  • The universal quantifier \(\forall\): "for all", "for every", "for each". \(\forall x \in \mathbb{R}\ (x^2 \ge 0)\) is true.
  • The existential quantifier \(\exists\): "there exists", "for some", "there is at least one". \(\exists x \in \mathbb{N}\ (x^2 = 49)\) is true (\(x = 7\)).

To show a universal statement is false, one counterexample is enough: "every prime is odd" is false because of 2. To show an existential statement is true, one example is enough.

Negation of quantified statements. To negate, switch the quantifier and negate what follows: \[ \neg\big(\forall x\, P(x)\big) \equiv \exists x\, \neg P(x), \qquad \neg\big(\exists x\, P(x)\big) \equiv \forall x\, \neg P(x). \] The negation of "All jeepney drivers have a license" is "Some jeepney driver does not have a license", not "No jeepney driver has a license". The negation of "Some students passed" is "No student passed" (every student did not pass). For connectives, De Morgan's laws hold: \(\neg(p \wedge q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg q\) and \(\neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q\).

Common mistake The opposite of "all" is not "none". If a teacher says "All of you passed" and that is false, it only means at least one student failed. Likewise the negation of "\(x > 5\)" is "\(x \le 5\)", not "\(x < 5\)".
Key equations
\[ \neg\forall x\,P(x) \equiv \exists x\,\neg P(x), \qquad \neg\exists x\,P(x) \equiv \forall x\,\neg P(x) \]
\[ \neg(p \wedge q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg q, \qquad \neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q \]

2.6Formality

Everyday conversation is informal: we rely on context, tone and shared experience. Mathematics, at its core, is formal: every term is defined, every assumption is stated, and every claim is justified by rules everyone can check. Formality is what lets a proof written in Manila in 2026 be checked and trusted by a mathematician in Tokyo or Nairobi.

The building blocks of formal mathematics.

  • Undefined terms: a few basic ideas taken as starting points (in geometry: point, line, plane; in set theory: set, element).
  • Definitions: precise meanings built from undefined terms and earlier definitions. A definition is an "if and only if": an integer \(n\) is even if and only if \(n = 2k\) for some integer \(k\).
  • Axioms (postulates): statements accepted as true without proof, such as "through two distinct points there is exactly one line".
  • Theorems: statements proved true from the axioms, definitions and earlier theorems by logical reasoning. A lemma is a helper theorem; a corollary follows quickly from a theorem.
  • Proofs: chains of logical steps showing that a theorem must be true.
Example Theorem. The sum of two even integers is even. Proof. Let \(m\) and \(n\) be even integers. By definition, \(m = 2a\) and \(n = 2b\) for some integers \(a, b\). Then \(m + n = 2a + 2b = 2(a + b)\). Since \(a + b\) is an integer, \(m + n\) is even by definition. \(\blacksquare\)

Notice how every step uses only the definition of "even" and the rules of algebra. Checking examples (\(2 + 4 = 6\), \(10 + 12 = 22\)) suggests the result but cannot prove it for all even integers; the formal proof does. You will compare these two ways of knowing, inductive and deductive reasoning, in Module 3.

Levels of formality. In practice mathematicians write at different levels: a fully formal proof (every step from axioms, as a computer proof checker would require), a standard written proof (like the one above, in sentences, skipping routine algebra), and informal explanations (pictures, examples, intuition) used to discover and teach ideas. All three are valuable, but only the formal levels establish truth.

Common mistake Using a word with its everyday meaning instead of its definition. "A function is a formula" is informal and wrong: a function is defined as a relation that pairs each input with exactly one output, and many functions (like the student-number function) have no formula at all.

★Key takeaways

  • The language of mathematics is precise (one meaning), concise (brief symbols) and powerful (ideas can be manipulated and generalized).
  • An expression names an object and has no truth value; a sentence makes an assertion. Closed sentences are true or false; open sentences contain a variable.
  • Conventions such as the order of operations (GEMDAS, left to right for ×÷ and +−) make every expression mean the same thing to every reader.
  • A set is a well-defined collection; n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B), and a set with n elements has 2ⁿ subsets.
  • A relation is a set of ordered pairs; a function is a relation in which every input has exactly one output.
  • A binary operation on A combines two elements of A into one element that is still in A.
  • Negating a quantified statement switches ∀ and ∃ and negates the rest: the opposite of 'all' is 'some … not'.
  • Formal mathematics is built from undefined terms, definitions and axioms, and its theorems are established by proof.

Example 2.1 From words to symbols

Let \(S\) be the set of students in your block, \(h(x)\) the height of student \(x\) in cm and \(w(x)\) the weight in kg. Write in symbols: (a) “Every student is shorter than 200 cm.” (b) “Some student weighs more than 80 kg.” (c) “There is a student who is taller than 180 cm and weighs at most 60 kg.” Then write the negation of (a) in symbols and in words.

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

“Every” becomes \(\forall\), “some” and “there is” become \(\exists\), “and” becomes \(\wedge\). “Shorter than” is \(<\); “at most” is \(\le\). To negate, switch the quantifier and negate the inner sentence.

Solution:

(a) \(\forall x \in S\ \big(h(x) < 200\big)\).

(b) \(\exists x \in S\ \big(w(x) > 80\big)\).

(c) \(\exists x \in S\ \big(h(x) > 180 \wedge w(x) \le 60\big)\).

Negation of (a): \(\exists x \in S\ \big(h(x) \ge 200\big)\): “Some student is at least 200 cm tall.”

Check:

The negation of \(<\) is \(\ge\), not \(>\): a student exactly 200 cm tall makes (a) false, so it must make the negation true. ✓

Example 2.2 Expressions, sentences and the order of operations

Classify each as an expression, an open sentence or a closed sentence; for closed sentences give the truth value. (a) \(12 - 8 \div 4 \times 2\) (b) \(12 - 8 \div 4 \times 2 = 8\) (c) \(5n + 2 > 17\) (d) \((2 + 3)^2 = 2^2 + 3^2\)

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Look for an asserting symbol (\(=\), \(>\), …). If there is one, check for a variable. Evaluate with GEMDAS: division and multiplication left to right.

Solution:

(a) No asserting symbol: an expression. Its value: \(8 \div 4 = 2\), \(2 \times 2 = 4\), \(12 - 4 = 8\).

(b) A closed sentence; by (a) the left side is 8, so it is true.

(c) Contains the variable \(n\): an open sentence (true exactly when \(n > 3\)).

(d) A closed sentence: \(25 = 4 + 9 = 13\) is false.

Check:

A common slip in (a) is to compute \(4 \times 2 = 8\) first and get \(12 - 8 \div 8 = 11\); multiplication and division go strictly left to right. Statement (d) is the classic error “the square of a sum is the sum of the squares”: the correct rule is \((a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2\).

Example 2.3 Operations on sets

Let \(U = \{1, 2, \dots, 12\}\), \(A = \{x \in U \mid x \text{ is even}\}\) and \(B = \{x \in U \mid x \text{ is a multiple of } 3\}\). Find \(A \cup B\), \(A \cap B\), \(A - B\), \(B'\), and verify \(n(A \cup B) = n(A) + n(B) - n(A \cap B)\).

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Write both sets in roster form first, then apply each definition element by element.

Solution:

\(A = \{2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12\}\), \(B = \{3, 6, 9, 12\}\).

\(A \cup B = \{2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12\}\); \(A \cap B = \{6, 12\}\); \(A - B = \{2, 4, 8, 10\}\); \(B' = \{1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11\}\).

\(n(A) + n(B) - n(A \cap B) = 6 + 4 - 2 = 8 = n(A \cup B)\). ✓

Check:

\(A \cap B\) should be the multiples of 6 in \(U\): \(\{6, 12\}\). ✓ \(B\) and \(B'\) together should have \(4 + 8 = 12 = n(U)\) elements. ✓

Example 2.4 Relations, functions and binary operations

(a) Which of these relations are functions? \(R_1 = \{(1, 4), (2, 5), (3, 4)\}\), \(R_2 = \{(1, 4), (1, 5), (2, 6)\}\), \(R_3 = \{(x, y) \mid y = 2x + 1, x \in \mathbb{R}\}\). (b) Is \(a * b = \frac{a}{b}\) a binary operation on \(\mathbb{N}\)? (c) Is \(a * b = a + b + 1\) a binary operation on \(\mathbb{N}\)? Compute \(4 * 5\).

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

For a function, check that no input has two outputs. For a binary operation, check that the result exists and stays in the set for every pair.

Solution:

(a) \(R_1\): inputs 1, 2, 3 each appear once, so it is a function (1 and 3 sharing the output 4 is allowed). \(R_2\): input 1 has two outputs, 4 and 5, so it is not a function. \(R_3\): each real \(x\) gives exactly one \(y = 2x + 1\), so it is a function.

(b) No: \(1 * 2 = \frac12 \notin \mathbb{N}\). One counterexample is enough.

(c) Yes: the sum of natural numbers plus 1 is a natural number. \(4 * 5 = 4 + 5 + 1 = 10\).

Check:

For (b), some pairs do work (\(6 * 3 = 2\)), but a binary operation must work for all pairs, so a single failure decides it.

Example 2.5 Negating quantified statements

Write the negation of each statement in words, and decide which is true, the statement or its negation. (a) “All prime numbers are odd.” (b) “There is a real number \(x\) with \(x^2 = -1\).” (c) “Some RTU students commute by MRT.”

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Use \(\neg\forall x\,P(x) \equiv \exists x\,\neg P(x)\) and \(\neg\exists x\,P(x) \equiv \forall x\,\neg P(x)\).

Solution:

(a) Negation: “Some prime number is not odd” (that is, some prime is even). The negation is true (2 is prime and even), so the statement is false.

(b) Negation: “For every real number \(x\), \(x^2 \ne -1\).” The negation is true, since \(x^2 \ge 0\) for every real \(x\).

(c) Negation: “No RTU student commutes by MRT” (every RTU student does not commute by MRT). The original is true (many students ride the MRT to Boni or Shaw station), so the negation is false.

Check:

Exactly one of each pair is true, as it must be for a statement and its negation. Note that the negation of (a) is not “All primes are even”.
