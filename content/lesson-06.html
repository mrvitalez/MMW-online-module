6.1Graphs and Euler Circuits

In the 1730s the city of Königsberg (now Kaliningrad, Russia) was built around the Pregel River, with two islands joined to the banks and to each other by seven bridges. Residents wondered whether one could take a walk that crosses every bridge exactly once. Leonhard Euler showed that it is impossible, and in doing so started a new branch of mathematics: graph theory.

Definition A graph is a set of points called vertices (or nodes) together with lines called edges that join pairs of vertices. A loop is an edge from a vertex to itself; multiple edges are two or more edges joining the same pair of vertices. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges that meet it (a loop counts 2).

Graphs model anything made of objects and connections: cities and roads, computers and network cables, people and friendships, courses that share students. Two vertices joined by an edge are adjacent. A graph is connected if you can travel from any vertex to any other along edges. A complete graph \(K_n\) has \(n\) vertices with every pair joined by exactly one edge.

The handshake fact. Each edge has two ends, so it adds 2 to the total degree. Therefore the sum of all the degrees is twice the number of edges. In particular, every graph has an even number of odd-degree vertices.

Key equations
\[ \sum \text{degrees} = 2 \times (\text{number of edges}) \qquad \text{edges of } K_n = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \]

Paths and circuits. A path is a sequence of vertices in which consecutive vertices are joined by edges. A circuit is a path that begins and ends at the same vertex. An Euler path uses every edge exactly once; an Euler circuit is an Euler path that returns to its starting vertex. The Königsberg walk would be an Euler path.

Euler's theorems. Every time a walk passes through a vertex, it uses one edge to arrive and one to leave, two at a time. So every vertex except the start and the end must have even degree.

Definition Euler circuit theorem: a connected graph has an Euler circuit if and only if every vertex has even degree. Euler path theorem: a connected graph has an Euler path (but no Euler circuit) if and only if it has exactly two odd vertices; the path must start at one odd vertex and end at the other.

In Königsberg all four vertices are odd, so there is neither an Euler circuit nor an Euler path. Adding or removing one bridge between two land masses would leave exactly two odd vertices and make a walk possible.

Fleury's algorithm finds an Euler path or circuit: start at an odd vertex (or anywhere if all are even); at each step, travel along any unused edge, but do not use a bridge of the remaining graph (an edge whose removal would disconnect the unused edges) unless there is no other choice; erase each edge after crossing it.

Hamiltonian circuits. A different question asks to visit every vertex exactly once and return to the start: a Hamiltonian circuit. Euler circuits are about edges, Hamiltonian circuits are about vertices. There is no simple test like Euler's for Hamiltonian circuits, but Dirac's theorem gives a sufficient condition: a connected graph with \(n \ge 3\) vertices, each of degree at least \(n/2\), has a Hamiltonian circuit. Every complete graph \(K_n\) (\(n \ge 3\)) has one.

Common mistake Mixing up Euler and Hamilton. A street sweeper or a mail carrier who must cover every street wants an Euler circuit. A delivery rider who must stop at every house once wants a Hamiltonian circuit. Also, the degree test only works for connected graphs.

6.2Weighted Graphs

In many problems the edges carry numbers: distances, travel times, fares or costs. A graph with a number (a weight) on each edge is a weighted graph. The weight of a path or circuit is the sum of the weights of its edges.

The traveling salesman problem. A delivery rider leaves the RTU campus in Mandaluyong, must drop parcels in Makati, Pasig, Quezon City and Manila, and must come back. Which order of stops gives the shortest total distance? This is the traveling salesman problem: find a Hamiltonian circuit of least total weight in a complete weighted graph.

Brute force. List every Hamiltonian circuit, add its weights, and pick the smallest. Starting from a fixed vertex, there are \((n-1)!\) orders of the other vertices, but each circuit is counted twice (once in each direction).

Key equations
\[ \text{number of distinct Hamiltonian circuits in } K_n = \frac{(n-1)!}{2} \]

For 5 stops that is \(4!/2 = 12\) circuits, easy to check by hand. For 10 stops it is 181,440; for 20 stops about \(6 \times 10^{16}\), too many even for a computer. So in practice we use quick approximation algorithms that give a good, though not always the best, circuit.

Nearest-neighbor algorithm. Start at the home vertex. At each step, go to the nearest vertex not yet visited. When all are visited, return home. From RTU in Figure 6.3: nearest is Pasig (5); from Pasig, nearest unvisited is Makati (8); then Manila (7); then QC (9); then back to RTU (9). Total \(5 + 8 + 7 + 9 + 9 = 38\) km.

Edge-picking (greedy) algorithm. List all edges from lightest to heaviest. Take edges in that order, skipping any edge that would give a vertex three edges or would close a circuit before every vertex is included. In Figure 6.3: take RTU–Pasig (5), RTU–Makati (6), Makati–Manila (7); skip Makati–Pasig (8, it would close a small circuit and give Makati three edges); take Manila–QC (9); skip RTU–QC (9, RTU already has two edges); skip Manila–RTU (10); take Pasig–QC (11), which completes the circuit. Total \(5 + 6 + 7 + 9 + 11 = 38\) km, the same circuit as before.

Checking all 12 circuits shows that 38 km is in fact the shortest here, and nearest-neighbor started from QC (QC–Manila–Makati–RTU–Pasig–QC, \(9 + 7 + 6 + 5 + 11 = 38\)) also finds it. This will not always happen: in other graphs (as in the activity) the quick methods can miss the best circuit by a little or by a lot. They trade a guarantee of the best answer for speed.

Common mistake Forgetting the return trip. A Hamiltonian circuit ends where it started, so the weight of the last edge back home must be added. Another common slip in edge-picking is accepting an edge that closes a circuit too early (before all vertices are included).

6.3Planarity and Euler’s Formula

A graph can be drawn in many ways: only the vertices and which pairs are joined matter, not the positions or the shapes of the edges. A graph is planar if it can be drawn in the plane with no edges crossing. Planarity matters for designing printed circuit boards (wires on one layer must not cross), subway maps and road interchanges.

A crossing in one drawing does not mean the graph is non-planar; you must show that no drawing avoids crossings. Two graphs are equivalent (isomorphic) if they have the same vertices joined in the same way, however they are drawn.

Faces and Euler's formula. A planar drawing divides the plane into regions called faces; the unbounded region outside the graph is also a face. Euler discovered that for every connected planar graph the numbers of vertices \(v\), edges \(e\) and faces \(f\) are tied together.

Key equations
\[ v - e + f = 2 \]
\[ e \le 3v - 6 \quad (\text{connected simple planar graph},\ v \ge 3) \]

Check Figure 6.4: \(v = 4\), \(e = 6\), \(f = 4\), and \(4 - 6 + 4 = 2\). A tree (a connected graph with no circuits) has only the outer face: \(v - (v-1) + 1 = 2\). The formula also tells you how many faces a planar drawing will have before you draw it: \(f = 2 - v + e\).

Proving a graph is not planar. In a simple planar graph every face is bounded by at least 3 edges and each edge borders at most 2 faces, which leads to the inequality \(e \le 3v - 6\). The complete graph \(K_5\) has \(v = 5\) and \(e = 10\), but \(3(5) - 6 = 9 \lt 10\), so \(K_5\) is not planar. The "utilities graph" \(K_{3,3}\) (three houses each joined to water, electricity and gas) is also non-planar; a similar argument uses the fact that it has no triangles, so \(e \le 2v - 4\), but \(9 \gt 2(6) - 4 = 8\).

Kuratowski's theorem says these two are the only obstacles: a graph is non-planar exactly when it contains a copy of \(K_5\) or \(K_{3,3}\), possibly with extra vertices inserted along the edges (a subdivision). So to show that a graph is not planar, find a \(K_5\) or \(K_{3,3}\) hidden inside it.

Common mistake Forgetting the outer face when counting faces, or applying \(e \le 3v - 6\) backwards. The inequality can prove that a graph is not planar (when it fails), but a graph that satisfies it is not automatically planar: \(K_{3,3}\) has \(9 \le 12\) yet is not planar.

6.4Graph Coloring

Map makers have long known that neighbouring regions should get different colours. If we place a vertex in each region and join two vertices whenever the regions share a border, the map becomes a graph, and colouring the map becomes colouring the vertices.

Definition A coloring of a graph assigns a colour to each vertex so that adjacent vertices get different colours. The smallest number of colours needed is the chromatic number of the graph. A graph that can be coloured with \(k\) colours is \(k\)-colorable.

The four-color theorem. Every planar graph, and so every map drawn on a plane, can be coloured with at most four colours. This was conjectured in 1852 and proved only in 1976 by Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken, with heavy use of a computer: one of the first major proofs that people could not check entirely by hand.

Useful facts about the chromatic number.

  • A graph with at least one edge needs at least 2 colours.
  • If the graph contains a triangle (3 mutually adjacent vertices), it needs at least 3 colours; if it contains \(K_4\), at least 4. In general \(K_n\) needs \(n\) colours.
  • Two-colorable graph theorem: a graph is 2-colorable if and only if it has no circuit with an odd number of vertices. For example, every tree and every even cycle is 2-colorable; a 5-cycle is not.

A greedy coloring method (Welsh–Powell idea). (1) List the vertices from highest to lowest degree. (2) Give the first vertex colour 1, then go down the list giving colour 1 to every vertex not adjacent to one already coloured 1. (3) Repeat with colour 2 on the uncoloured vertices, and so on. This does not always give the minimum, but it is quick and often does.

Application: scheduling exams. Six departmental exams must be scheduled so that no student has two exams at the same time. Make each exam a vertex and join two exams when some student takes both. Exams with the same colour can share a time slot, so the chromatic number is the minimum number of time slots.

In Figure 6.6, Math–Physics–Chem is a triangle, so at least 3 slots are needed, and the colouring shown uses exactly 3: slot 1 for Math and English, slot 2 for Physics and History, slot 3 for Chem and Drawing. The same idea assigns radio frequencies to transmitters, registers to variables in a compiler, and rooms to classes.

Common mistake Thinking that a vertex with many edges forces many colours. The chromatic number depends on how the vertices are linked, not on the largest degree: a star with one centre joined to 20 outer vertices needs only 2 colours.

★Key takeaways

  • A graph keeps only the connections: vertices and the edges joining them; the sum of all degrees equals twice the number of edges.
  • A connected graph has an Euler circuit exactly when every vertex is even, and an Euler path (not a circuit) exactly when it has two odd vertices, which are the ends of the path.
  • Euler circuits use every edge once; Hamiltonian circuits visit every vertex once.
  • The complete graph Kₙ has (n − 1)!/2 distinct Hamiltonian circuits, so brute force quickly becomes impossible and approximation algorithms are used.
  • Nearest neighbor and edge picking give good circuits quickly, but not always the best one.
  • A connected planar graph satisfies v − e + f = 2; K₅ and K₃,₃ are not planar.
  • The chromatic number is the least number of colours with adjacent vertices different; every planar map needs at most four colours, and a graph is 2-colorable exactly when it has no odd circuit.

Example 6.1 Degrees and Euler paths

A graph has vertices \(P, Q, R, S, T\) and edges \(PQ, PR, QR, QS, RS, RT, ST\). (a) Find the degree of each vertex and check the handshake fact. (b) Does the graph have an Euler circuit? An Euler path? If so, find one.

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Count the edges at each vertex. Then apply Euler’s theorems: all even \(\Rightarrow\) Euler circuit; exactly two odd \(\Rightarrow\) Euler path from one odd vertex to the other.

Solution:

(a) \(P\): \(PQ, PR\), degree 2. \(Q\): \(PQ, QR, QS\), degree 3. \(R\): \(PR, QR, RS, RT\), degree 4. \(S\): \(QS, RS, ST\), degree 3. \(T\): \(RT, ST\), degree 2. Sum \(= 2 + 3 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 14 = 2 \times 7\) edges.

(b) Exactly two vertices, \(Q\) and \(S\), are odd. So there is no Euler circuit, but there is an Euler path from \(Q\) to \(S\). One is \(Q\text{–}P\text{–}R\text{–}Q\text{–}S\text{–}R\text{–}T\text{–}S\).

Check:

The path lists 8 vertices, so it uses 7 edges: \(QP, PR, RQ, QS, SR, RT, TS\). Each of the 7 edges appears exactly once, and it starts and ends at the two odd vertices, as the theorem requires.

Example 6.2 Nearest neighbor versus the best route

A barangay health worker starts at the health centre \(H\) and must visit households \(A, B, C\) and return. Travel times in minutes: \(HA = 4\), \(HB = 6\), \(HC = 9\), \(AB = 3\), \(AC = 8\), \(BC = 5\). (a) How many different Hamiltonian circuits are there? (b) Use the nearest-neighbor algorithm from \(H\). (c) Find the optimal circuit by brute force.

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

With \(n = 4\) vertices there are \((4-1)!/2 = 3\) circuits, few enough to list. For nearest-neighbor, always choose the cheapest edge to an unvisited vertex, then return home.

Solution:

(a) \(3!/2 = 3\) circuits.

(b) From \(H\): nearest is \(A\) (4). From \(A\): \(B\) (3) beats \(C\) (8). From \(B\): only \(C\) is left (5). Back to \(H\) (9). Circuit \(H\text{–}A\text{–}B\text{–}C\text{–}H\), total \(4 + 3 + 5 + 9 = 21\) minutes.

(c) The three circuits are \(H\text{–}A\text{–}B\text{–}C\text{–}H = 4 + 3 + 5 + 9 = 21\); \(H\text{–}A\text{–}C\text{–}B\text{–}H = 4 + 8 + 5 + 6 = 23\); \(H\text{–}B\text{–}A\text{–}C\text{–}H = 6 + 3 + 8 + 9 = 26\). The optimal circuit is \(H\text{–}A\text{–}B\text{–}C\text{–}H\), 21 minutes.

Check:

Each of the 6 edges appears in exactly two of the three circuits, so the three totals should add to twice the sum of all weights: \(21 + 23 + 26 = 70 = 2(4 + 6 + 9 + 3 + 8 + 5)\). Here nearest-neighbor happened to find the optimum.

Example 6.3 Using Euler’s formula

(a) A connected planar graph has 8 vertices and 12 edges. How many faces does any planar drawing of it have? (b) Show that a simple graph with 7 vertices and 16 edges cannot be planar.

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Use \(v - e + f = 2\) for (a). For (b), test the necessary condition \(e \le 3v - 6\).

Solution:

(a) \(f = 2 - v + e = 2 - 8 + 12 = 6\) faces (including the outer face). The cube is an example: 8 corners, 12 edges, 6 faces.

(b) If it were planar, then \(e \le 3v - 6 = 3(7) - 6 = 15\). But \(e = 16 \gt 15\). So the graph is not planar.

Check:

For (a), the cube’s skeleton can be drawn flat as a small square inside a big square with corners joined: count 5 bounded regions plus the outer one, 6 faces, matching the formula.

Example 6.4 Coloring a map of regions

Five barangays share borders as follows: \(A\) borders \(B, C, D\); \(B\) borders \(A, C, E\); \(C\) borders \(A, B, D, E\); \(D\) borders \(A, C, E\); \(E\) borders \(B, C, D\). Find the chromatic number of the border graph, and give a colouring.

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Look for triangles to get a lower bound, then try to colour with that many colours, starting with the highest-degree vertex.

Solution:

\(A, B, C\) are mutually adjacent (a triangle), so at least 3 colours are needed. Colour \(C\) (degree 4) red. \(A\), \(B\), \(D\), \(E\) all touch \(C\), so none is red. \(A\) blue, then \(B\) (touches \(A\)) green, \(D\) (touches \(A\)) green, \(E\) (touches \(B\) and \(D\), both green) blue. Check \(B\) and \(D\): they are not adjacent, so sharing green is fine. The chromatic number is 3.

Check:

Go through all 8 borders: \(AB\) blue/green, \(AC\) blue/red, \(AD\) blue/green, \(BC\) green/red, \(BE\) green/blue, \(CD\) red/green, \(CE\) red/blue, \(DE\) green/blue. No border has the same colour on both sides.
