5.1Logic Statements and Quantifiers

Logic is the part of mathematics that studies reasoning itself: which conclusions follow from which assumptions, and why. In Module 2 you met the connectives and quantifiers as part of the language of mathematics. In this module you will use them as tools: to translate everyday sentences into symbols, to decide when two sentences say the same thing, and to test whether an argument is valid.

Definition A statement (or proposition) is a declarative sentence that is either true or false, but not both. Its truth value is T (true) or F (false).

"Manila is the capital of the Philippines" is a statement (true). "RTU is in Cebu" is also a statement (false): being false does not stop it from being a statement. On the other hand, "Close the door", "Is the MRT running today?", "Wow!" and "This sentence is false" are not statements. The first three are a command, a question and an exclamation; the last one cannot be consistently true or false. An open sentence such as \(x + 3 = 7\) is not a statement either, until a value of \(x\) is given or a quantifier is attached.

Simple and compound statements. A simple statement expresses one idea. A compound statement is built from simple statements using connectives. We name simple statements with lowercase letters \(p, q, r, \ldots\)

ConnectiveSymbolNameTypical English words
not\(\neg p\)negationnot, it is not the case that
and\(p \wedge q\)conjunctionand, but, yet, although, however
or\(p \vee q\)disjunctionor (inclusive: one or both)
if ... then\(p \rightarrow q\)conditionalif p then q; p implies q; q if p; p only if q
if and only if\(p \leftrightarrow q\)biconditionalp if and only if q; p iff q
Example Let \(p\): "It is raining" and \(q\): "Classes are suspended." Then "It is raining but classes are not suspended" is \(p \wedge \neg q\) (the word but is a conjunction). "If it is not raining, then classes are not suspended" is \(\neg p \rightarrow \neg q\). "It is not the case that it is raining and classes are suspended" is \(\neg(p \wedge q)\): the parentheses show that the negation applies to the whole conjunction.

Grouping. In English, commas do the job of parentheses. "Either it is raining and I bring an umbrella, or I stay home" is \((p \wedge u) \vee h\), while "It is raining, and I bring an umbrella or I stay home" is \(p \wedge (u \vee h)\). When translating, decide first which connective is the main one, the one that joins the largest pieces.

Quantifiers. Many statements talk about a whole collection: "All engineering students take GE04", "Some jeepneys are electric", "No square is a triangle". The words all, every, each, no, none are universal quantifiers (\(\forall\)); the words some, there exists, at least one are existential quantifiers (\(\exists\)). In logic, "some" means at least one, possibly all.

Negating quantified statements. To show that "All jeepney drivers wear seatbelts" is false, you need only one driver who does not. So the negation is "Some jeepney drivers do not wear seatbelts", not "No jeepney driver wears a seatbelt".

Key equations
\[ \neg(\text{All } A \text{ are } B) \equiv \text{Some } A \text{ are not } B \qquad \neg(\text{Some } A \text{ are } B) \equiv \text{No } A \text{ are } B \]
\[ \neg\big(\forall x,\; P(x)\big) \equiv \exists x,\; \neg P(x) \qquad \neg\big(\exists x,\; P(x)\big) \equiv \forall x,\; \neg P(x) \]
Common mistake The negation of "All students passed" is not "All students failed". It only takes one student who did not pass to make the original false, so the negation is "Some students did not pass". Likewise, the negation of "Some students passed" is "No student passed".

5.2Truth Tables and Tautologies

The truth value of a compound statement depends only on the truth values of its parts. A truth table lists every possible combination of truth values of the simple statements and gives the truth value of the compound statement in each case.

The basic tables. The negation flips the truth value. A conjunction is true only when both parts are true. A disjunction is false only when both parts are false (the "or" of logic is inclusive).

\(p\)\(q\)\(\neg p\)\(p \wedge q\)\(p \vee q\)
TTFTT
TFFFT
FTTFT
FFTFF

How many rows? Each simple statement has 2 possible truth values, so a statement with \(n\) different simple statements needs \(2^n\) rows: 2 rows for one statement, 4 for two, 8 for three, 16 for four. To list them systematically, the first column alternates in blocks of \(2^{n-1}\) T's and F's, the next in blocks half as long, and the last column alternates T, F, T, F, ...

Order of precedence. When parentheses are missing, apply negation first, then conjunction, then disjunction, then the conditional, then the biconditional. So \(\neg p \vee q\) means \((\neg p) \vee q\), and \(p \wedge q \rightarrow r\) means \((p \wedge q) \rightarrow r\). When in doubt, write the parentheses.

Example Build the table of \((p \vee q) \wedge \neg r\). There are three simple statements, so 8 rows. Work column by column: first \(p \vee q\), then \(\neg r\), then join them with "and".
\(p\)\(q\)\(r\)\(p \vee q\)\(\neg r\)\((p \vee q) \wedge \neg r\)
TTTTFF
TTFTTT
TFTTFF
TFFTTT
FTTTFF
FTFTTT
FFTFFF
FFFFTF

The compound statement is true in exactly 3 of the 8 rows: those where \(r\) is false and at least one of \(p, q\) is true.

Equivalent statements. Two statements are logically equivalent, written \(\equiv\), when they have the same truth value in every row of the truth table. Equivalent statements say the same thing in different words, which is useful when you want a simpler or clearer form.

Key equations
\[ \neg(p \wedge q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg q \qquad \neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q \qquad \neg(\neg p) \equiv p \]

These are De Morgan's laws. In words: "It is not true that I passed Math and Physics" means "I failed Math or I failed Physics (or both)". "It is not true that I take the jeep or the MRT" means "I take neither the jeep nor the MRT".

Tautologies and self-contradictions. A tautology is a statement that is true in every row, for example \(p \vee \neg p\) ("Either it rains or it does not rain") or \((p \wedge q) \rightarrow p\). A self-contradiction is false in every row, for example \(p \wedge \neg p\). Two statements are equivalent exactly when the biconditional joining them is a tautology.

Common mistake Distributing the negation without switching the connective. \(\neg(p \wedge q)\) is not \(\neg p \wedge \neg q\). Check one row: if \(p\) is T and \(q\) is F, then \(\neg(p \wedge q)\) is T but \(\neg p \wedge \neg q\) is F. De Morgan's laws always swap "and" with "or".

5.3The Conditional and the Biconditional

The conditional "if \(p\), then \(q\)" is the most important connective in mathematics, because every theorem has this form: if the hypotheses hold, then the conclusion holds. In \(p \rightarrow q\), \(p\) is the antecedent (hypothesis) and \(q\) is the consequent (conclusion).

When is a promise broken? Suppose your instructor says: "If you submit all activities, then you will get a bonus point." The only way the instructor broke the promise is if you submitted all activities (T) and did not get the bonus (F). If you did not submit all activities, the promise says nothing about you, so it is not broken, whether or not you got a bonus. This gives the truth table:

\(p\)\(q\)\(p \rightarrow q\)\(\neg p \vee q\)
TTTT
TFFF
FTTT
FFTT
Definition The conditional \(p \rightarrow q\) is false only when the antecedent is true and the consequent is false. In every other case it is true.

The last two rows surprise many students: a conditional with a false antecedent is true ("vacuously true"). For example, "If \(2 + 2 = 5\), then RTU is in Mandaluyong" is a true statement.

The conditional as a disjunction. Comparing tables shows that \(p \rightarrow q\) is false in exactly the same row as \(\neg p \vee q\). So the two are equivalent, and this lets us negate a conditional with De Morgan's law:

Key equations
\[ p \rightarrow q \equiv \neg p \vee q \qquad \neg(p \rightarrow q) \equiv p \wedge \neg q \]
\[ p \leftrightarrow q \equiv (p \rightarrow q) \wedge (q \rightarrow p) \]

So the negation of "If it rains, then classes are suspended" is "It rains and classes are not suspended". Notice that the negation of a conditional is not another conditional.

The biconditional. \(p \leftrightarrow q\) ("\(p\) if and only if \(q\)") means both \(p \rightarrow q\) and \(q \rightarrow p\). It is true when \(p\) and \(q\) have the same truth value and false when they differ:

\(p\)\(q\)\(p \leftrightarrow q\)
TTT
TFF
FTF
FFT

Definitions in mathematics are biconditionals: "A triangle is equilateral if and only if its three sides are equal". In everyday speech people often say "if" when they mean "if and only if", but in logic the two are different.

Common mistake Writing the negation of "If I study, then I pass" as "If I study, then I do not pass" or "If I do not study, then I do not pass". The correct negation is "I study and I do not pass": it describes the one situation that makes the original conditional false.

5.4The Conditional and Related Statements

From one conditional \(p \rightarrow q\) we can form three related statements by swapping and/or negating its parts.

NameFormExample (\(p\): a number is divisible by 4, \(q\): it is even)
Conditional\(p \rightarrow q\)If a number is divisible by 4, then it is even. (T)
Converse\(q \rightarrow p\)If a number is even, then it is divisible by 4. (F: 6 is even)
Inverse\(\neg p \rightarrow \neg q\)If a number is not divisible by 4, then it is not even. (F: 6 again)
Contrapositive\(\neg q \rightarrow \neg p\)If a number is not even, then it is not divisible by 4. (T)

The example shows a general fact, which a truth table confirms:

\(p\)\(q\)\(p \rightarrow q\)\(q \rightarrow p\)\(\neg p \rightarrow \neg q\)\(\neg q \rightarrow \neg p\)
TTTTTT
TFFTTF
FTTFFT
FFTTTT
Key equations
\[ p \rightarrow q \equiv \neg q \rightarrow \neg p \qquad q \rightarrow p \equiv \neg p \rightarrow \neg q \]

A conditional and its contrapositive are equivalent: they are always both true or both false. The converse and the inverse are equivalent to each other, but not to the original. This is why mathematicians often prove a theorem by proving its contrapositive instead, when that is easier.

Other ways to say "if \(p\), then \(q\)". All of the following mean \(p \rightarrow q\):

  • \(q\) if \(p\). ("You get a bonus if you submit everything.")
  • \(p\) only if \(q\). ("You may enrol in GE04 only if you are a registered RTU student" means: enrolled \(\rightarrow\) registered.)
  • \(p\) is sufficient for \(q\). (Being divisible by 4 is sufficient for being even.)
  • \(q\) is necessary for \(p\). (Being even is necessary for being divisible by 4.)
  • Every \(p\) is \(q\). / All \(p\) are \(q\). (Every number divisible by 4 is even.)

The phrase "only if" is the trickiest. "\(p\) only if \(q\)" says that \(p\) cannot happen without \(q\); in other words, if \(p\) happens then \(q\) has happened. The "only if" part points to the consequent.

Common mistake Assuming that a true conditional has a true converse. "If you live in Mandaluyong, then you live in Metro Manila" is true; its converse "If you live in Metro Manila, then you live in Mandaluyong" is false. Never replace a statement by its converse or inverse without checking.

5.5Symbolic Arguments

An argument consists of statements called premises and a statement called the conclusion. The argument is valid if the conclusion is true whenever all the premises are true; otherwise it is invalid (a fallacy).

Validity is about the form of the argument, not about whether the premises are actually true. An argument with a false premise can still be valid. An argument that is valid and has true premises is called sound; its conclusion is guaranteed to be true.

Definition The argument with premises \(p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n\) and conclusion \(c\) is valid exactly when the conditional \((p_1 \wedge p_2 \wedge \cdots \wedge p_n) \rightarrow c\) is a tautology.

Testing an argument with a truth table. (1) Write the argument in symbols. (2) Build a truth table with a column for each premise and for the conclusion. (3) Look only at the rows where every premise is true. If the conclusion is true in all of those rows, the argument is valid; if it is false in even one, the argument is invalid.

Example "If it rains, the game is cancelled. The game is cancelled. Therefore it rained." In symbols: \(p \rightarrow q,\; q,\; \therefore p\).
\(p\)\(q\)\(p \rightarrow q\)\(q\)\(p\)
TTTTT
TFFFT
FTTTF
FFTFF
In the third row both premises are true but the conclusion is false (the game may have been cancelled for another reason). The argument is invalid.

Standard forms. Some forms appear so often that it pays to recognise them on sight.

Valid formPremisesConclusion
Modus ponens (direct reasoning)\(p \rightarrow q,\; p\)\(q\)
Modus tollens (contrapositive reasoning)\(p \rightarrow q,\; \neg q\)\(\neg p\)
Disjunctive syllogism (disjunctive reasoning)\(p \vee q,\; \neg p\)\(q\)
Hypothetical syllogism (transitive or chain rule)\(p \rightarrow q,\; q \rightarrow r\)\(p \rightarrow r\)
Invalid form (fallacy)PremisesConclusion
Fallacy of the converse (affirming the consequent)\(p \rightarrow q,\; q\)\(p\)
Fallacy of the inverse (denying the antecedent)\(p \rightarrow q,\; \neg p\)\(\neg q\)

For example: "If the signal number is 3 or higher, classes are suspended. The signal number is 4. So classes are suspended" is modus ponens (valid). "If a phone is waterproof, it costs over 20,000 pesos. This phone does not cost over 20,000 pesos. So it is not waterproof" is modus tollens (valid, though a premise may be false in real life). The chain rule lets you link many conditionals: if \(p \rightarrow q\), \(q \rightarrow r\), \(r \rightarrow s\), then \(p \rightarrow s\).

Common mistake Judging an argument by whether you agree with its conclusion. "If a number is divisible by 6, it is even. 8 is even. Therefore 8 is divisible by 6" is invalid (fallacy of the converse) and here the conclusion is also false. But the same invalid form can produce a true conclusion by luck; it is still invalid because the premises do not guarantee it.

5.6Arguments and Euler Diagrams

Arguments whose premises use the quantifiers all, some, no are easier to test with pictures than with truth tables. An Euler diagram represents each set as a closed region (usually a circle). Unlike a Venn diagram, an Euler diagram shows only the relationships that the premises allow.

The method. (1) Draw a diagram that makes every premise true. (2) Check whether the conclusion is true in that diagram. (3) Look for any other drawing that also makes every premise true but makes the conclusion false. If such a drawing exists, the argument is invalid. If every possible drawing forces the conclusion, the argument is valid.

Example "All engineers are problem solvers. Ana is an engineer. Therefore Ana is a problem solver." The first premise puts the engineers circle inside the problem solvers circle; the second puts Ana's dot inside the engineers circle. There is no way to draw this without Ana inside the problem solvers circle, so the argument is valid.
Example "All engineers are problem solvers. Ben is a problem solver. Therefore Ben is an engineer." This time the premises only place Ben's dot somewhere in the problem solvers region. He could be inside the engineers circle, or outside it. Because one drawing makes the conclusion false, the argument is invalid (it is the fallacy of the converse in disguise).

Arguments with "some". Be careful: "some" only promises one element in an overlap; it does not say where the other elements are. "Some students are athletes. Some athletes are singers. Therefore some students are singers" is invalid: we can draw the students circle and the singers circle both overlapping the athletes circle without overlapping each other.

Arguments with "no". "No reptiles are mammals. All snakes are reptiles. Therefore no snakes are mammals." Draw reptiles and mammals apart; the snakes circle must sit inside reptiles, so it cannot touch mammals. The argument is valid.

Common mistake Drawing only one diagram and stopping when the conclusion looks true. To call an argument valid you must be sure that no diagram satisfying the premises makes the conclusion false. Actively try to "break" the conclusion before you accept it.

★Key takeaways

  • A statement is either true or false; questions, commands and open sentences are not statements.
  • The negation of "all A are B" is "some A are not B", and the negation of "some A are B" is "no A are B".
  • A truth table with n simple statements has 2ⁿ rows; equivalent statements agree in every row.
  • De Morgan's laws: ¬(p ∧ q) ≡ ¬p ∨ ¬q and ¬(p ∨ q) ≡ ¬p ∧ ¬q.
  • A conditional p → q is false only when p is true and q is false; its negation is p ∧ ¬q.
  • A conditional is equivalent to its contrapositive, but not to its converse or inverse.
  • An argument is valid when the conclusion is true in every row where all premises are true; modus ponens, modus tollens, the chain rule and disjunctive syllogism are valid, while the fallacies of the converse and inverse are not.
  • With Euler diagrams, an argument is invalid if you can draw the premises in a way that makes the conclusion false.

Example 5.1 Translating and negating

Let \(p\): "The MRT is running" and \(q\): "I will be late." (a) Write in symbols: "The MRT is not running, or I will not be late." (b) Write in words: \(\neg(p \vee q)\) and simplify it with De Morgan’s law. (c) Write the negation of "Some RTU students commute by bicycle."

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Find the main connective in each sentence first. For (b) apply De Morgan: the negation moves inside and "or" becomes "and". For (c), the negation of "some" is "no".

Solution:

(a) "The MRT is not running" is \(\neg p\), "I will not be late" is \(\neg q\), joined by "or": \(\neg p \vee \neg q\).

(b) \(\neg(p \vee q)\) reads "It is not the case that the MRT is running or I will be late." By De Morgan, \(\neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q\): "The MRT is not running and I will not be late."

(c) "Some RTU students commute by bicycle" claims at least one such student. Its negation says there are none: "No RTU student commutes by bicycle."

Check:

A truth table confirms (b): the two columns agree in all four rows.
\(p\)\(q\)\(\neg(p \vee q)\)\(\neg p \wedge \neg q\)
TTFF
TFFF
FTFF
FFTT
For (c), the original and the negation must always have opposite truth values, and they do: if at least one student bikes, the "no" statement is false, and if none do, the "some" statement is false.

Example 5.2 A truth table and a tautology

Show that \([(p \rightarrow q) \wedge p] \rightarrow q\) is a tautology. What does this tell you about modus ponens?

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

With two simple statements, the table has \(2^2 = 4\) rows. Build the inner pieces first, then the main conditional. Remember: a conditional is false only in the case T \(\rightarrow\) F.

Solution:

\(p\)\(q\)\(p \rightarrow q\)\((p \rightarrow q) \wedge p\)\([(p \rightarrow q) \wedge p] \rightarrow q\)
TTTTT
TFFFT
FTTFT
FFTFT

The last column is T in every row, so the statement is a tautology.

This statement is exactly "(premise 1 and premise 2) implies conclusion" for the argument \(p \rightarrow q,\; p,\; \therefore q\). Since it is a tautology, modus ponens is a valid argument form.

Check:

The only way the main conditional could be false is if \((p \rightarrow q) \wedge p\) were T and \(q\) were F. But if \(p\) is T and \(q\) is F, then \(p \rightarrow q\) is F, so the antecedent is F. That case can never happen, which confirms the table.

Example 5.3 Converse, inverse and contrapositive

Consider "If a polygon is a square, then it has four sides." Write its converse, inverse and contrapositive, and give the truth value of each. Then rewrite the original using "only if" and using "necessary".

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Let \(p\): "the polygon is a square" and \(q\): "it has four sides". Use the forms \(q \rightarrow p\), \(\neg p \rightarrow \neg q\), \(\neg q \rightarrow \neg p\). The contrapositive has the same truth value as the original; the converse and inverse share a truth value with each other.

Solution:

Original \(p \rightarrow q\): If a polygon is a square, then it has four sides. True.

Converse \(q \rightarrow p\): If a polygon has four sides, then it is a square. False (a rectangle that is not a square, or a trapezoid).

Inverse \(\neg p \rightarrow \neg q\): If a polygon is not a square, then it does not have four sides. False (the same rectangle).

Contrapositive \(\neg q \rightarrow \neg p\): If a polygon does not have four sides, then it is not a square. True.

"A polygon is a square only if it has four sides." "Having four sides is necessary for a polygon to be a square."

Check:

The pattern T, F, F, T matches the rule: original \(\equiv\) contrapositive, converse \(\equiv\) inverse. The single counterexample (a non-square rectangle) breaks both the converse and the inverse at once, as it should.

Example 5.4 Testing an argument

Determine whether the argument is valid. "If I oversleep, I miss the jeepney. If I miss the jeepney, I am late for GE04. I was not late for GE04. Therefore I did not oversleep."

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Write it in symbols with \(p\): I oversleep, \(q\): I miss the jeepney, \(r\): I am late. The premises are \(p \rightarrow q\), \(q \rightarrow r\), \(\neg r\); the conclusion is \(\neg p\). Either recognise standard forms or use a truth table.

Solution:

Using standard forms: by the chain rule, \(p \rightarrow q\) and \(q \rightarrow r\) give \(p \rightarrow r\). Then \(p \rightarrow r\) and \(\neg r\) give \(\neg p\) by modus tollens. The argument is valid.

Using a truth table:
\(p\)\(q\)\(r\)\(p \rightarrow q\)\(q \rightarrow r\)\(\neg r\)\(\neg p\)
TTTTTFF
TTFTFTF
TFTFTFF
TFFFTTF
FTTTTFT
FTFTFTT
FFTTTFT
FFFTTTT
Only the last row has all three premises true, and there \(\neg p\) is T. So the argument is valid.

Check:

Think about it backwards: if I had overslept, I would have missed the jeepney and then been late. I was not late, so I could not have overslept. The everyday reasoning agrees with the formal test.

Example 5.5 Euler diagrams

Use Euler diagrams to decide whether each argument is valid. (a) "No typhoons are harmless. Some storms are typhoons. Therefore some storms are not harmless." (b) "All jeepneys are vehicles. Some vehicles are electric. Therefore some jeepneys are electric."

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Draw the premises, then try to find a drawing that keeps every premise true but makes the conclusion false.

Solution:

(a) "No typhoons are harmless": draw the typhoons and harmless circles apart. "Some storms are typhoons": the storms circle overlaps typhoons, with a dot \(x\) in the overlap. That storm \(x\) is a typhoon, so it lies outside the harmless circle. Every drawing has such a storm, so "some storms are not harmless" is forced. Valid.

(b) "All jeepneys are vehicles": jeepneys inside vehicles. "Some vehicles are electric": the electric circle overlaps vehicles. But the electric circle can overlap the part of vehicles outside the jeepneys circle (think of electric cars and buses). In that drawing no jeepney is electric. Invalid.

Check:

For (b), the conclusion may happen to be true in real life (there are e-jeepneys), but the premises do not guarantee it, so the argument is still invalid. Validity is about whether the conclusion is forced, not about whether it is true.
